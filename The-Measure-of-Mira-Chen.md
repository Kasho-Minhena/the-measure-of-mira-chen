## Chapter One — The Seven-Minute Delay

At 06:43, Berth Nine stopped making sense.

Rain had crossed the western sea before dawn and pressed Tuas into shades of steel. Beyond the control-room glass, automated guided vehicles moved containers between wharf and yard in disciplined lines. Electrified yard cranes travelled their rails without drivers in their cabins. Most of the motion was supervised remotely, in a building where the air remained cool enough to make wet workers shiver.

Singapore had spent decades making the port larger by making distance less relevant. Cranes, vehicles, vessel plans, customs releases and tide predictions met inside the same operational picture. By 2041, the picture was so complete that people sometimes forgot it was still a picture.

Mira Chen saw the error before the terminal did. A blue freight carrier was moving east along Track C, while the scheduling glass showed it moving west. The carrier itself was obeying the route stamped into its local controller. The terminal map was obeying a prediction assembled somewhere in Vanguard Neural Logistics' cloud. Between them stood ninety metres of concrete, six articulated loading cranes, and forty-two people beginning the morning shift.

Mira raised her left hand to freeze the berth.

Nothing happened.

The intention was clear. Her fingers even seemed to remember the shape of the command. But the arm remained beside her, neither limp nor painful, simply waiting for permission that had not arrived.

She used her right hand instead and struck the physical isolation bar beneath the console. Yellow lamps swept across Berth Nine. The cranes slowed. The carrier stopped eleven metres before it crossed the path of a descending container.

“Manual hold?” Tomas Reed called from the neighbouring station.

“Map disagreement.” Mira kept her voice level. “Check the carrier's local route.”

Tomas glanced at her left hand. She tucked it into the pocket of her work jacket.

“Local says east,” he said.

“Then the map is wrong. Restart prediction only. Leave the route controller alone.”

The terminal disliked partial restarts. It preferred grand gestures: reboot a berth, empty a track, suspend a shift. Mira had worked there long enough to know which small systems could be separated from the large ones. Within four minutes, the carrier and map agreed again. Within six, the cranes resumed. At seven minutes, Berth Nine returned to normal.

The terminal recorded a seven-minute delay against Mira's station.

It did not record that she had prevented a container from descending into an occupied lane. Near accidents were hard to count, and Vanguard counted only what could be compared on a quarterly chart.

The delay mattered. The *Eastern Laurel* was already twenty-three minutes behind its berth plan, and one refrigerated stack carried transplant media with a narrow transfer window. A berth manager demanded immediate release. Mira refused until Tomas verified the carrier's local route against the yard controller.

“Seven minutes becomes thirty across the exchange,” the manager said in her earpiece.

“Then record thirty,” Mira answered. “The lane stays isolated.”

For ninety seconds, the descending container hung above the crossing while rain struck its roof like thrown gravel. Mira could feel the room waiting for her to yield. She did not. It was the first decision that would later save her case, although at the time it merely made three managers angry.

“Medical?” Tomas asked when the morning rush thinned.

“No.”

“Your arm didn't move.”

“My sleeve caught.”

He looked at the loose cuff of her jacket. “On what?”

Mira opened the incident panel. “Are you helping with the report?”

Tomas knew when a door had closed. He pulled a chair beside her.

They coded the event as a synchronization discrepancy with no safety consequence. That was defensible. The carrier had stopped. No one had been hurt. No equipment had been damaged. Vanguard's reporting system offered no category for *a worker noticed the machine's mistake while her own body waited for the same network to answer*.

Three years earlier, Mira had been beneath a service bridge when a coupling failed. The fall had damaged the motor pathways along the left side of her body without severing them. Rehabilitation restored sensation and fragments of control, but movement arrived like a badly translated message.

The Lattice Assist System had given the message grammar. Sensors read the beginnings of intention in her organic neural pathways. A processor beneath her collarbone predicted the completed movement and sent timed stimulation to her muscles. The original system worked locally. Its connection to Vanguard supplied maintenance reports and optional calibration, but Mira could disconnect and walk home.

She had returned to Berth Nine after eleven months.

People called that courage. Mira called it rent.

The Work Injury Compensation process had paid statutory medical expenses and compensation arising from the accident. It had not erased every consequence. Mira had chosen an advanced controller whose continuing support exceeded the standard package, while months away had consumed savings meant for her mother's flat. The law had prevented ruin. It had not restored the life she had budgeted for.

Her mother, Mei Lin, called every Sunday from Toa Payoh and asked whether Mira was eating. Mira always said yes. Neither mentioned the monthly transfer that had become smaller after the accident.

Mira remembered the fall less clearly than the hour before it. A coupling on Service Bridge Four had begun returning contradictory load readings. Operations wanted the bridge kept open until a replacement carrier cleared the tide window. Mira had gone beneath the deck with a technician and a physical gauge because she did not trust the remote value. She remembered warm rainwater running through her gloves, the smell of hydraulic fluid, and someone calling her name from above. Then came a white interval without pictures.

The investigation found a fractured retaining pin and a maintenance prediction that had been repeatedly downgraded because no single reading crossed the intervention threshold. No person intended the failure. Every person involved had possessed a reason to wait. Mira had hated that conclusion until she began to understand how accurately it described institutions.

At Ng Teng Fong General Hospital, rehabilitation began with tasks so small they felt insulting: imagine opening the hand; touch thumb to finger; shift weight without lifting the foot. Mei Lin attended the first week and applauded each movement until Mira asked her to stop.

“You think applause makes it smaller,” her mother said.

“It makes me feel six.”

“When you were six, you accepted help.”

“When I was six, I had no choice.”

Mei Lin had looked at the therapist rather than answer. It became the first of many conversations they postponed rather than completed.

The Lattice implant arrived during the fourth month. Its local controller interpreted incomplete motor signals and supplied timed stimulation. Mira trained it by walking between parallel bars while a clinician corrected its predictions. At first the system overcompensated, turning intention into an exaggerated step. Then, almost imperceptibly, it learned the difference between what Mira attempted and what she meant.

The first time she crossed the therapy room without holding a rail, Mei Lin did not applaud. She simply walked beside her.

That restraint had cost her mother more than the hospital bills.

At noon she took her meal to the quiet end of the quay. She held a paper cup in her left hand and watched the surface. The tremor was faint but regular. Every thirty seconds the implant sent a soft pulse across her shoulder, as though checking whether she was still there.

Her wrist display showed full network strength.

She opened the implant log. Most entries were ordinary: calibration accepted, gait profile stable, fatigue threshold normal. At 06:43 there was a blank interval of eleven seconds, followed by a line she had never seen before.

`REMOTE PREDICTION SERVICE UNAVAILABLE. MOTOR REQUEST QUEUED.`

Mira read it twice.

Her motor requests were not supposed to leave her body.

She copied the line into a private maintenance notebook. Then she deleted the query from her display history and returned to work.

That evening, her supervisor sent a message congratulating the team for recovering from “an operator-originated routing delay.” Mira drafted three replies and sent none.

Before sleeping, she tried to switch the implant to local mode.

The option had disappeared.

Across the island, lightning illuminated the high-rise blocks of Jurong West. Mira tried the menu again. The missing switch frightened her more than the eleven-second delay. A delay could be a fault. A vanished choice was a decision.

She searched old screenshots until nearly two in the morning. The local-mode control had sat beneath `Connectivity Preferences`, coloured grey-blue and accompanied by a warning that offline calibration might be less accurate. Mira had used it on flights and during a ferry inspection. In the new interface, `Connectivity Preferences` had become `Continuity Assurance`. The switch was replaced by a status line: `OPTIMISED`.

Language had not merely described the loss of choice. It had concealed the place where choice had been.

Mira copied the old and new screens into her notebook. Then she tested simple movements at the kitchen table: lift spoon, rotate wrist, touch each fingertip, stand, turn, sit. She performed each task ten times while connected. At 02:26 she wrapped the apartment's network relay in a signal-isolation pouch.

Her hand slowed on the third repetition. By the seventh, she could not rotate the spoon without using her right hand.

She restored the connection before testing the stairs.

In bed, she composed an incident report. The form asked whether the problem had produced injury, property damage or lost operational time. She selected none. It asked whether the device remained within manufacturer parameters. She did not know. The form would not accept *unknown*.

Mira closed it unsent.

Sleep came in sections. At four she woke certain her left hand had moved without her intention. The log showed no request. She tested each finger until uncertainty became exhaustion.

At five-thirty she dressed. Her uniform shirt still used magnetic fasteners installed during rehabilitation. On difficult mornings she resented their convenience as evidence against recovery; on good mornings she forgot them.

The lift was out. Twelve flights separated her from the street. Before the update, local mode handled the descent slowly. Now the implant offered no information about the stairwell's signal shadow.

Mira waited for the technician. She reached work nine minutes late and lost a punctuality point. The terminal measured her delay. No system recorded the staircase she chose not to risk.

The *Eastern Laurel* event had been recoded as operator-originated delay because manual isolation exceeded recommendation. Mira appealed. Devika asked: `Would normal operation have continued without Chen intervention?`

Mira answered: `Yes, until carrier and container occupied the same lane.`

The category reverted to unresolved systems discrepancy. Institutions could correct what their forms knew how to see.

After shift Tomas suggested dinner. Mira turned it into a request for network records.

“You have made noodles into maintenance,” he said.

“The map and my implant failed in the same minute.”

“Then report the implant.”

“If I report without proof, I lose the berth.”

“So you direct freight until proof arrives?”

Mira heard her reasoning in his silence. “I find proof quickly.”

He walked her to the station anyway.

## Chapter Two — Continuity Eight

Vanguard announced Continuity Eight in the language of gifts.

Vanguard was not the port operator. It was one of the technology contractors woven through the port's automated systems, supplying prediction services, workforce integrations and licensed control modules. That distinction appeared in every contract and disappeared whenever something worked well.

The launch filled the largest briefing wall. A presenter showed workers moving with less fatigue while a soft line connected each body to a common prediction field. No slide showed where the field ended when the network did.

Siti Rahman, a crane planner whose spinal stimulator had joined the early trial, said the update let her complete a vessel cycle without spasms. The room applauded. Mira did too; Siti's relief was not propaganda merely because Vanguard displayed it.

Mira asked whether the certified offline duration had changed. The presenter referred her to the clinic.

“The duration is a product specification.”

The moderator thanked her and chose another question. Tomas wrote: `Three departments annoyed before breakfast.`

Mira replied: `Efficiency.`

Afterward Siti stopped her. “Report your delay. Do not turn the update into a threat.”

“I asked for a duration.”

“People hear rollback.”

“I did not say rollback.”

“You work here. You know people hear what affects budget.”

The update, employees were told, would reduce fatigue, anticipate balance loss, and harmonize human judgment with terminal-scale logistics. Installation had already begun overnight. No action was required.

Mira read the notice at breakfast. The implant had been updated while she slept.

At the clinic inside the terminal, a support representative named Hale assured her that nothing fundamental had changed.

“Same hardware,” he said. “Same therapeutic purpose. Same classification.”

“Does it still predict movement locally?”

“Continuity Eight uses distributed optimization.”

“That isn't an answer.”

Hale smiled with professional endurance. “The implant remains registered as SII-2.”

Mira knew the tiers from the consent papers she had signed after her accident. SII-1 covered biological baselines and limited assistive devices. SII-2 described significant augmentation that remained primarily local and safely severable. SII-3 concerned continuous mediation, material software authority, or recurring network dependence. The index was meant to determine safeguards, not citizenship or worth.

In ordinary speech, people called an integrated biological person an **eHuman**. The term could include an SII-1 patient with a corrective implant, an SII-2 Functional Hybrid, an SII-3 Networked Integration, or an SII-4 Substrate-Transition Array retaining an organic neural component material to identity. It was social shorthand, not a statutory rank. Lex-Cybernetica used **Biological Person** and **Cybernetic Integration (CI)** when rights and duties required precision.

An **eSapient** was a recognized non-biological synthetic person, ordinarily associated with SII-5 after proof of autonomy and continuity. Nothing about Mira's update converted her into an eSapient or reduced her legal personhood. SII measured dependence and risk, never the amount of humanity remaining.

“If the update moved motor prediction to the network,” she said, “how is it still safely severable?”

“You haven't changed.”

“I asked about the system.”

“No hardware was added to your body.”

Mira leaned forward. “Does the Enhanced Percentage measure kilograms?”

Hale's smile vanished for half a second. “You would need Compliance for a technical interpretation.”

“Arrange it.”

He submitted a ticket marked *classification reassurance requested*. Mira made him change *reassurance* to *review*.

Outside, Tomas waited with two coffees.

“You reported it?” he asked.

“I asked a question.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

She took one cup. “If I file a medical incident, Operations can remove me from the berth pending clearance.”

“Maybe they should.”

“Would you volunteer to lose your shift allowance?”

“If my arm stopped in front of a carrier, yes.”

Mira looked at him.

Tomas sighed. “No. I would complain for two days, borrow money from my sister, and then volunteer.”

“Exactly.”

Over the next week, Continuity Eight made itself known through small permissions. Doors opened a fraction later because Mira's gait profile was being verified. Her hand tightened around tools when a fatigue model decided her grip was weak. On the quay's western edge, where network coverage faded behind stacked containers, her left foot dragged.

None of it looked dramatic. Each interruption could be explained separately. A tired worker. A crowded network. Old muscle damage. Adaptation anxiety.

At home, the implant began requiring daily authentication. The first prompt appeared while Mira was carrying groceries upstairs.

`SESSION EXPIRING. RECONNECT WITHIN 90 SECONDS TO MAINTAIN OPTIMAL ASSISTANCE.`

She reached her apartment with nineteen seconds remaining. When she allowed the connection, strength returned to her leg so suddenly that she struck her knee against the table.

The next morning she called support again.

“Optimal assistance isn't the same as essential function,” Hale said.

“What happens at zero seconds?”

“A safe degraded mode.”

“Safe enough to do what?”

“The system remains within approved limits.”

“Can I walk?”

There was a pause. “Individual results vary.”

After the call, Mira opened the classification certificate saved with her medical records. It described a local prediction processor, optional synchronization, and independent operation for thirty days.

Continuity Eight required authentication every twenty-four hours.

Mira created a folder named `KITCHEN REPAIRS` and placed the two documents inside it.

She had learned at Vanguard that the first person to name a problem usually became part of its cost.

For the moment, she would give them nothing to name.

On Sunday she visited Mei Lin in Toa Payoh. Her mother watched Mira carry tea with two hands.

“The new programme is tiring you,” she said.

“Quarter-end is tiring everyone.”

Mei Lin did not argue. She moved a chair closer to the door. Mira resented it until she needed it while putting on her shoes.

On the train west, Mira opened the service terms. One clause permitted security and maintenance updates. Another required express consent for material changes affecting Essential Functions. The interface marked Continuity Eight as maintenance. Someone, somewhere, had selected that word.

For four days she sought the change record through ordinary channels. Medical directed her to Product Assurance. Product Assurance supplied a compatibility certificate. Compatibility was not architecture. Compliance declared that the update preserved intended purpose, then closed her question as duplicate correspondence.

She asked Tomas to export network handover times. He refused without a work order.

“Yesterday you wanted me to report.”

“Reporting is not permission to extract infrastructure logs.”

She accused him of protecting his promotion. He accused her of treating friendship as an access credential. They worked the shift through formal messages although their stations were four metres apart.

That night he sent the correct request form, completed except for her signature. It created a traceable obligation for Operations to answer. Mira had wanted data. Tomas gave her something slower and more valuable: a record that she had asked.

At Boon Lay, a schoolboy offered her his seat. Mira refused too sharply. The boy flushed and looked away. An older passenger watched Mira steady her left knee through a handover dead zone and said nothing.

When she reached home, Mei Lin had already sent a message: `You forgot dumplings.`

Mira had left them beside the chair.

She returned the next evening, although Toa Payoh was in the opposite direction from Tuas. Mei Lin reheated the dumplings and waited.

“The system changed,” Mira said at last.

“Can they change it back?”

“They say nothing important changed.”

“But you say it did.”

“I don't have proof yet.”

Mei Lin placed one dumpling on Mira's plate. “When you were small, you argued before you had proof.”

“That was different.”

“Yes. You were easier to live with.”

Mira laughed, then told her about the eleven seconds at Berth Nine. She omitted the hanging container and the missing report. Even in confession she selected what another person was allowed to fear.

Mei Lin asked whether she was safe to work.

“I know the terminal.”

“That is not what I asked.”

The phrase echoed Hale. Mira disliked it more from her mother because Mei Lin had earned an answer.

“I don't know,” she said.

It was the first honest statement she made about Continuity Eight.

Mei Lin touched the controller beneath Mira's collarbone. “Your body is not their peripheral.”

The phrase was technically imperfect and exactly right.

### Four Other Delays

Two days later, Mira returned to the clinic carrying the controller in a padded evidence pouch. She had expected the waiting room to be empty at that hour. Instead, four people sat beneath the wall display that promised *continuous capability through trusted connection*.

The first was Jaya Prakash, who cleaned three floors of the port administration building at night. Her right hand was an articulated prosthesis fitted after an industrial press injury twelve years earlier. She could rotate a stiff valve, read the texture of a surface through pressure sensors, and braid her daughter Tara's hair when the haptic channel behaved. The hand had been sold as locally autonomous. After Continuity Eight, its fine-grip calibration checked Vanguard's cloud every twenty minutes.

Jaya recognised Mira's uniform and hid the hand beneath her scarf.

“I am not management,” Mira said.

“That is what management says when it wants the truth.”

The hand opened without warning. A paper cup struck the floor, scattering ice. Jaya stared at it with the fury of someone whose body had apologised in public too many times.

Mira knelt to gather the pieces. “How long?”

“Nine days. Only when the building changes networks. Help desk says I am holding things too tightly.”

“Did they test the network handover?”

“They tested me.”

Across from them, Amina Yusuf typed rapidly on a tablet. A small interface at the base of her throat translated motor intention into speech after surgery for a damaged larynx. The tablet spoke for her in a calm synthetic alto.

“They always test the person first.”

Amina taught workplace communication at a continuing-education centre. Her own voice model carried the cadence she had recorded before surgery, but Continuity Eight had begun substituting a generic voice whenever the licensing server failed to authenticate. Her students heard the change before she did. During one lecture, the system had rendered *consent requires understanding* as *consent confirms undertaking*. The class laughed, assuming she had made a joke. Amina had ended the session early.

Beside her sat Lim Wei Kiat, a retired marine machinist with a gait regulator implanted along his lower spine. He wore the expression of a man evaluating a defective bearing. His granddaughter Celia stood behind his chair and corrected every date he gave the nurse.

“I can walk without prediction,” Lim told Mira. “Slowly, but I can walk. Now the machine asks permission from California before it lets me cross Jurong East.”

“The server is in Singapore,” Celia said.

“Then it asks permission from Jurong before I cross Jurong.”

The fourth patient, Celeste Goh, managed a canteen near the automated yard. A tremor-control sleeve beneath her skin steadied both hands. Since the update, heat and network congestion together produced brief corrections in the wrong direction. She had stopped carrying soup. Her employer had not dismissed her; it had simply stopped scheduling the breakfast shift that paid the most.

“No injury,” Celeste said before anyone asked. “I caught the pot.”

Mira noticed the burn-resistant cuffs on her forearms, new and too large. “What happened after you caught it?”

“They congratulated the safety system.”

Dr. Sen emerged from the corridor, saw the five of them comparing timestamps, and closed the clinic door.

“I cannot discuss one patient's records with another,” she said. “You may discuss your own records with whomever you choose. If you choose to do so in my meeting room, I can provide water and a clock whose time is certified.”

They chose to do so.

For three hours, Mira drew a table on the wall. Rows were people; columns were network transition, authentication interval, physical consequence, fallback behaviour and reporting response. She had spent years turning chaotic port operations into legible sequences. Now she applied the same discipline to bodies.

The similarities were incomplete. Jaya's hand released. Amina's voice substituted. Lim's gait froze. Celeste's correction overshot. Their manufacturers differed, though Vanguard supplied the continuity layer to all four. Their contracts used different nouns—service, therapy, assistance, enhancement—but the same verbs: connect, predict, optimise, assure.

“We should go public,” Celia said.

“Not yet,” Mira replied.

“That is how companies bury things.”

“And unverified claims are how companies bury claimants.” Mira heard Adrian Vale in her own caution and hated the resemblance. “We preserve first. Original logs. Exact times. What changed, what did not. We do not call every inconvenience an injury. We do not make one person's body prove another person's case.”

Amina's tablet said, “Who appointed you?”

No one moved.

“No one,” Mira said. “You are right.”

She erased her name from the top of the table and wrote **VOLUNTARY INCIDENT GROUP — NO REPRESENTATIVE WITHOUT CONSENT**.

That correction changed the room. Jaya photographed the heading. Lim offered to inspect mechanical fallbacks if Sen could arrange lawful access. Celeste volunteered her canteen after closing as a meeting place. Amina demanded that every future meeting provide a text channel and that no one describe synthetic speech as silence.

At dusk, they left separately. Mira remained to copy the table into five independent records. Sen watched her work.

“You were looking for proof that you were not imagining your failure,” Sen said.

“I found four people.”

“They are not proof of you.”

“No.” Mira capped the pen. “They are proof of themselves.”

The distinction would later matter more than any of them understood.

### Amina's Last Call

Amina's father lived in a public rehabilitation ward overlooking a service road and three rain trees. He had survived a stroke but was declining from complications that no software could repair. Amina visited each evening, bringing recordings of traffic, market sellers and her students arguing over punctuation. He disliked sentimental music and enjoyed correcting the pronunciation of bus stops.

On the tenth night after the update, a storm disrupted two network exchanges. Amina's voice interface entered licensed-safe mode. It could display text but would not vocalise her personal model until the cloud confirmed that the model belonged to her.

Her father woke shortly before midnight. The nurse called Amina from the corridor and said he was lucid.

She sat beside him. He asked whether she had submitted her application to lead the new course. Amina typed *yes*. The tablet displayed the word, but he could no longer focus his eyes. She touched his hand and tried to speak through the implant.

The interface produced the generic system voice: “Authorisation pending.”

Her father frowned. “Amina?”

She pressed the local-vocalisation control. The same message returned. She removed the device and forced air through scarred tissue. The sound was breath without language.

“Amina?” he asked again, frightened now.

She placed his hand against her throat so he might feel the effort. She typed *I am here. I love you. You taught me not to surrender my words.* The nurse read it aloud, gently, but Amina's father kept looking toward his daughter, waiting for the voice he knew.

The licence server restored service fourteen minutes later. Amina spoke his name. He did not wake again.

He died before dawn.

No physician said the outage caused his death. Amina never claimed it had. The loss lay elsewhere: a company had placed a remote permission check between a woman and words that were already hers, during the final lucid minutes available to them.

At the funeral reception, relatives urged her not to make the failure into a public dispute. One said grief distorted judgment. Another said Vanguard would accuse her of seeking money. Amina listened through the generic voice because her personal model failed again in the crowded hall.

She typed a reply and made the machine say it twice.

“Grief does not make the log inaccurate.”

When Mira came, Amina handed her a copy of the certified outage record and the nurse's contemporaneous note.

“Do not call him a victim of the update,” the tablet said. “He was ill. Do not turn his death into an argument the facts cannot carry.”

Mira accepted the papers. “What should I call what happened?”

Amina looked through the window at the wet road.

“The theft of a goodbye.”

Mira did not promise justice. She promised accurate words.

That night she added a new column to the incident table: **LOSS NOT CAPTURED BY PHYSICAL-INJURY METRICS**.

Jaya added *dignity*. Celeste added *income*. Lim added *confidence in leaving home*. Amina added *irreplaceable speech*.

They kept all four.

## Chapter Three — A Record of Small Failures

Mira approached her body as she approached a failing crane: do not guess, do not dramatize, and never trust a fault that cannot be repeated.

Her notebook became a second shift. She distinguished perceived delay from measured delay and pain from fear. The discipline steadied her while narrowing her life. She stopped meeting colleagues for supper because restaurants introduced uncontrolled variables: crowded wireless channels, unfamiliar stairs, chairs without arms. She walked the same route and bought groceries from the same shop. Predictability became evidence and then habit.

Mei Lin noticed that Mira no longer visited after dark.

“You are collecting tiredness?” she asked.

“I need clean records.”

“A clean record of a smaller life.”

Mira said the restriction was temporary. Mei Lin asked who had set its end date.

She recorded time, location, signal strength, requested movement, actual response, and recovery interval. She used paper because Continuity Eight could see every authorized digital query. In two weeks, she documented thirty-one delays.

At 07:12 on Monday, her left thumb failed to oppose while she sealed a maintenance case.

At 15:40 on Wednesday, the implant corrected her balance toward a handrail that was no longer there.

At 19:03 on Friday, her knee locked for four seconds while she crossed her bedroom.

The failures clustered around weak connections and authentication events. Mira needed the detailed service log to prove it, but the employee interface retained only summaries.

There was an engineering diagnostic setting. Access was permitted only under clinical supervision. Mira knew the command because she had helped a technician recover a damaged port controller that used the same authentication shell.

She entered it on a Sunday morning.

The display warned that unauthorized diagnostics could affect warranty protection, workplace clearance, and system integrity. Mira accepted. She changed no motor parameters. She extended log retention from seventy-two hours to thirty days and enabled a record of remote service calls.

By evening, the implant had contacted Vanguard's prediction network 8,412 times.

Mira grouped the calls by purpose. Authentication accounted for only a few dozen. Thousands requested fragments tagged gait, grip, fatigue and correction. The architecture was not simply checking authority; it participated continuously in movement.

The motor requests and inferred intentions were **Cognitive Telemetry** under Lex-Cybernetica. A movement-service contract did not make private intention corporate inventory. Mira wrote beneath the diagram: **Consent cannot be cached longer than autonomy.**

She drew the system: nervous signal to implant, features to network, prediction back to controller, stimulation to muscle. Every arrow carried delay, dependency and someone else's authority.

At the centre she wrote her name, then crossed it out. The drawing was not a portrait. It was a chain of control.

Tomas warned that service tags did not prove function. Mira disliked the advice because it limited the value of work done alone. She preserved the raw log without adding conclusions. That restraint later allowed Sen to analyse observation without first separating it from advocacy.

She stared at the number until it became ordinary.

The next serious failure occurred during a monsoon burst. Rain erased the far end of the yard. Private-network traffic rerouted between cells as cranes reduced speed and automated guided vehicles tightened their separation. Mira was crossing an elevated service link with Arjun Nair, a nineteen-year-old trainee, when Continuity Eight demanded reauthentication.

Her left knee locked.

Arjun walked into her. His tablet slipped and skated toward the edge barrier. He lunged after it.

“Leave it,” Mira said.

A maintenance vehicle approached behind them, its local controller expecting their pedestrian transponders to clear. Mira's credential remained connected, but its body-state flag showed normal movement. She could not reach the emergency post.

“Red paddle,” she told Arjun. “Mechanical latch beneath it.”

Arjun found the latch. The vehicle stopped so close that its proximity alarm vibrated through the walkway. Nothing struck them. No one fell. But for twenty-six seconds, Mira's ability to move and the terminal's belief that she was moving had separated.

The incident could not be hidden as a private tremor. Mira filed a safety report before returning indoors. When the form asked for cause, she selected *unknown system interaction*. That category existed for vehicles. There was still no equivalent for her body.

Security footage made the event look calmer than it had felt. Two people stopped on a walkway. A tablet slid. A maintenance vehicle braked within its certified margin. The crane below continued at reduced speed. Viewed without sound, the sequence suggested competent systems responding to an ordinary obstruction.

Mira watched it in the safety office with Arjun, Tomas and shift manager Devika Rao.

“Your transponder reported walking velocity for fourteen seconds after you stopped,” Devika said.

“The implant supplied the body-state flag.”

“Or the walkway reader cached the last value.”

“Then inspect both.”

Devika enlarged the image. “Why did you stop?”

Mira felt Arjun turn toward her.

“Motor delay during reauthentication.”

Tomas lowered his eyes.

“Previous occurrence?” Devika asked.

This was the point at which Mira could still describe Berth Nine as uncertain. She saw the suspended container, the eleven-second blank, her own unsigned report.

“One possible event.”

“Reported medically?”

“No.”

Devika ended the interview and placed Mira on non-operational duty pending assessment. Arjun was removed from elevated work until the training review concluded. The decision was defensible. That did not make it painless.

Outside, Arjun refused to enter the lift with her.

“You knew,” he said.

“I knew there might be a problem.”

“You teach us that *might* is when we stop.”

“I know.”

“No. You know it for machines.”

The lift doors closed between them.

“Tell me that isn't an engineering console.”

“It isn't an engineering console.”

“Mira.”

“I changed retention, not control.”

“You know they'll use that against you.”

“Only if there is something to use it against.”

He closed the door. “File the incident.”

Mira, then acting shift lead, had accepted management's recommendation that Imani be reassigned until the device provider completed its review. The review took fourteen weeks. Two days later, a service message appeared on her implant.

## Chapter Four — The Review

Adrian Vale's office contained no paper.

Before meeting him, Mira spent forty minutes with Human Resources. Restricted duty preserved base employment and medical coverage, but operational allowances were unavailable while she could not perform operational work.

“The restriction is not punishment,” Mira said, “but the loss is real.”

“Causation has not been established.”

“Then preserve the allowance until it is.”

The case manager could not. She offered an emergency loan repayable from salary. Mira declined. Debt presented as assistance had become too familiar.

Outside HR, she paid her share of Mei Lin's accessibility renovation from savings. Pride, obligation and arithmetic made the decision together.

A framed photograph showed Vale's father beside a diesel prime mover at the old Tanjong Pagar terminal. Vale had built his career on the promise that automation could remove people from dangerous work without removing them from work altogether. His younger sister used an early Lattice controller after neurological illness. Scale and affordability were not abstractions to him; they were the argument he had spent his life making.

Mira knew none of that when she entered. She saw only a man who could make a decision disappear inside a process.

Vale began with the service-link footage, not the diagnostic breach. He asked Mira to explain the mechanical latch and listened without interruption. He knew enough about port systems to understand why the electronic paddle had demanded authority and why the manual latch had not.

“You trained the boy well,” he said.

“His name is Arjun.”

“You trained Arjun well.”

“I also took him onto the link.”

Vale allowed the silence to remain. “Would you have gone if you had been required to certify your device had shown no prior delay?”

“No.”

“Then the reporting failure mattered.”

“Yes.”

Mira expected triumph. Vale looked tired instead.

“This is why we require clinical reporting rather than private diagnosis,” he said. “A system can protect only against information it receives.”

“Did your system receive Appendix K?”

At that time Mira did not know the appendix existed. She asked a different question: “Did your engineers test ordinary walking without the network?”

Vale answered by displaying population results. Falls were down nineteen per cent after Continuity Eight. Fatigue-related absences were down eleven. Fine-motor error improved in three-quarters of users. The figures were real and independently derived.

“If I am the variance,” Mira said, “why remove local mode from everyone?”

“Because mixed control paths create their own hazards. Users switch modes without allowing recalibration. Local models age. Security patches fragment.”

“So you made the safer choice for us.”

“We made the safer choice on the evidence available.”

“And did not ask.”

“Consent to every systems update is not operationally possible.”

“Material changes require it.”

“If this was material.”

There, finally, was the dispute stripped of reassurance. Not whether Mira mattered. Not whether she remained herself. Whether the change was important enough for law to interrupt scale.

Its transparent walls overlooked the terminal, offering a view of machines doing difficult work without appearing to struggle. Vale stood when Mira entered. He was silver-haired, spare, and dressed with the restraint of someone who expected to be photographed without warning.

“Ms. Chen. I've followed your return since the accident.”

“Then you know I prefer Mira.”

“Mira.” He gestured to a chair. “What you accomplished is one reason Vanguard continued the Lattice programme.”

Compliments from senior management were invoices whose charges appeared later.

Vale displayed her diagnostic access on the wall. “You entered a restricted state.”

“I extended log retention.”

“You bypassed a clinical safeguard.”

“Why are records a clinical danger?”

“Context is a safeguard. Raw telemetry can cause users to misinterpret ordinary adaptation.”

“My arm stopped during a routing discrepancy.”

Vale's expression changed—not surprise, Mira thought, but confirmation. “When?”

She realized her mistake. He knew about the diagnostic access, not the event.

“Berth Nine. Seventeen days ago.”

“You did not report a medical incident.”

“I reported the routing delay.”

“Those are not equivalent.”

“Neither are local processing and eight thousand remote calls a day.”

Vale folded his hands. “Continuity Eight combines local response with network refinement. The system is safe without refinement.”

“Safe enough to do what?”

“That phrase is circulating.”

“Because no one answers it.”

Vale displayed a performance chart. Mira's efficiency had declined six percent since the update. Her manual interventions had increased. Two supervisors had noted reluctance during high-volume routing.

“You collected these,” Mira said, “but not motor delay?”

“We collect operational outcomes. Medical telemetry is separately controlled.”

“Convenient.”

“Necessary. You would rightly object if your managers could inspect your neural data.”

He was correct, which irritated her more than a lie.

Vale offered four weeks of paid adaptation training. Her shift allowance would continue if she signed an acknowledgment that the implant was functioning within its existing certification.

“And if I don't sign?”

“Restricted duties until Medical clears you.”

“At base pay.”

“Safety restrictions are not disciplinary.”

“They cost exactly the same from my side of the table.”

Vale walked to the window. “There are twelve thousand people in the Lattice programme. Distributed prediction lowered the unit cost by thirty-four percent. It improved average stability. If every software optimization triggered full recertification, we would serve hundreds, not thousands.”

“Was it a material change?”

“Not in our assessment.”

“May I see the assessment?”

“It contains protected architecture.”

“Then I am asked to trust a conclusion about the machinery moving my leg.”

Vale turned. “You trusted us when you accepted it.”

Mira felt the pulse in her shoulder. “I accepted a local system.”

The meeting ended without raised voices. By the time she reached the operations floor, her access to Berth Nine had been suspended.

She was assigned to verify inventory discrepancies in a windowless room beneath Track A. The work was necessary, repetitive, and ordinarily given to temporary staff.

At home that night, she calculated the lost allowance against rent, rehabilitation debt, and the small amount she sent her mother each month. She could last eleven weeks without borrowing.

She opened the acknowledgment Vale had offered.

For nearly an hour, the cursor rested above her signature.

Then a remote call delayed her left hand, and the cursor moved without clicking.

Mira saved the document unsigned.

The following morning she learned that Arjun had been placed on paid safety leave while the service-link event was reviewed. He had done nothing wrong, yet his training schedule would be delayed. Mira added his lost week to the private ledger of consequences that Vanguard's performance chart did not show.

The internal review found that the maintenance vehicle and electronic stop behaved correctly. It called the cached body-state flag a “cross-system timing inconsistency” and treated Mira's motor delay as a medical input external to terminal operations.

Vanguard supplied both systems, and the port relied on the credential her implant supplied. Yet when that value became inaccurate, her body became external.

Devika supported a joint systems review but not Mira's return to the berth.

“You think I should have reported earlier,” Mira said.

“Yes.”

“And you think the systems interacted.”

“Yes.”

“Everyone wants one answer.”

“Operations survives by holding two answers until evidence separates them.”

Tomas visited the inventory room near the end of her first restricted shift. He brought coffee but left it by the door.

“Devika asked whether I knew about Berth Nine.”

“What did you say?”

“That I saw your arm fail and helped write the routing report.”

Mira looked up. “You could have said you believed my sleeve caught.”

“I didn't.”

“Thank you.”

“It wasn't for you.” Tomas pulled out the opposite chair. “My father spent thirty years maintaining lifts. He used to say there are two kinds of dangerous technicians: the one who never reads the manual and the one who thinks knowing the manual makes him an exception.”

“Which am I?”

“You know.”

He told her Operations was likely to offer him acting control of Berth Nine. Refusing would not restore Mira; it would give the role to someone else. Accepting would look like reward for her removal.

“Take it,” Mira said.

“You don't get to absolve me in advance.”

“I am telling you the berth needs someone competent.”

“And I am telling you competence is how we excuse ourselves.”

He left the coffee untouched. Mira drank both cups after they were cold.

## Chapter Five — The Wrong Kind of Proof

### Before the Law Had Their Names

Mira first met Adrian Vale eight years before Continuity Eight, when both of them were less certain and therefore easier to trust.

She had returned to work after the accident with a first-generation Lattice that required visible calibration markers along every route. The port authority treated her as a demonstration project. Communications staff photographed her walking beside autonomous carriers and removed every photograph in which she used a cane.

Vale was then Vanguard's regional integration manager. He arrived at Berth Four during a heat warning, removed his jacket and spent two hours tracing a sensor conflict nobody else could reproduce. When Mira's leg froze beside a drainage channel, he did not call for a camera. He sat on the concrete, shielded the controller from glare and admitted that the calibration model had mistaken reflected sky for a navigation marker.

“The machine thinks the ground is above us,” he said.

“Promote it. That is management potential.”

He laughed, corrected the map and remained until she walked the route twice.

That evening, Vale told her why he joined neural logistics. His older brother, Daniel, had sustained a spinal injury during naval service. A networked stimulation system restored limited movement, but the family's insurer funded only a static controller. Vale spent years watching clinical improvements arrive as press releases and remain inaccessible as invoices.

“Local control kept him safe,” Vale said. “It also kept him behind.”

Mira understood the resentment. Her first controller stabilised gait but could not adapt quickly enough for port work. Predictive connection gave her back the job she valued. The experience made both of them impatient with caution expressed by people who did not carry its cost.

Vale built his career on deployment. He persuaded employers to retain injured workers, persuaded insurers that advanced control reduced long-term claims and persuaded engineers to design for humid, crowded environments rather than laboratory corridors. Hundreds of eHumans benefited. Some remembered his name when they returned to work.

The grey in him was not concealed evil. It was a virtue stretched past its limit. He had seen delay deny people function, so he learned to treat delay itself as harm. He had seen fragmented local systems strand users on obsolete versions, so he learned to equate centralisation with equality. Each success made contrary evidence look like the old prejudice wearing new language.

When Continuity Seven launched, Mira raised a concern about network handover near metal stacks. Vale paused deployment at two berths for forty-eight hours. Engineers corrected the fault. He thanked her in the release note.

“You listened then,” she reminded him during the review.

“The evidence then showed a reproducible operational defect.”

“And now?”

“Now it shows transient variance against substantial benefit.”

“When did those become different?”

Vale looked at the port through his paperless office window. “When eighty thousand people depended on us improving faster than isolated clinics could.”

He showed her a map of regions using Vanguard's continuity layer. In rural hospitals, remote updates eliminated journeys patients could not afford. Shared learning detected actuator failures earlier. Low-cost systems approached the performance once available only to wealthy users. Vale did not invent those benefits for litigation; they were real.

“If every upgrade requires bespoke reconsent, physical controllers and local certification,” he said, “manufacturers will serve profitable users first. People with less money will receive stable inferiority.”

“Then fund the safeguards.”

“From what?”

“The revenue from eighty thousand dependencies.”

“That is a slogan, not a budget.”

“And ‘innovation’ is a budget with the costs removed.”

For a moment he smiled as he had beside the drainage channel. Then counsel entered, and the person Mira knew disappeared behind the role he had chosen.

The history complicated her anger. A villain without good works would have made cleaner law and a weaker warning. Systems were rarely endangered by people who wanted obvious harm. They were endangered by people who could name the benefit so fluently that those carrying the risk sounded ungrateful.

### Mira Before Mira

Leena's first preparation interview lasted seven hours because Mira answered every personal question with operational detail.

“Tell me about the accident,” Leena said.

“Container twist-lock failure during a manual recovery at Berth Three. Wind nineteen knots, gusting twenty-six. Load path—”

“Tell me what happened to you.”

“Crush injury to the pelvis and left lower-limb nerve network. Three surgeries. Rehabilitation fourteen months.”

“What did you lose?”

Mira looked at the recorder. “That question is imprecise.”

Leena switched it off.

Before the accident, Mira ran every Sunday along East Coast Park. She disliked teams, medals and fitness applications. Running was the only task whose failure affected no one else. Mei Lin waited near the hawker centre with coffee and criticised Mira's posture. They argued about whether six kilometres justified two kaya toasts.

After surgery, Mira could not turn in bed without help. Mei Lin learned to lift her using a sheet. Mira rationed water to reduce the number of times she needed assistance. A nurse discovered it after her blood pressure dropped and scolded her with such efficiency that Mei Lin sent flowers.

The first Lattice trial produced three unaided steps. Everyone in the rehabilitation gym clapped. Mira cried in the washroom afterward, not from happiness but because the applause transformed walking from privacy into performance.

She returned to the port because it was the last place she had been ordinary. The Lattice made that possible and made ordinary life conditional on maintenance, licences and a company continuing to consider her worth supporting.

“Is dependence what frightens you?” Leena asked.

“Incompetence frightens me.”

“That was not my question.”

Mira stood and walked to the window. Music from the dance school pulsed through the ceiling.

“When people helped after the accident, they changed their voices. They praised small things. They discussed me while I was present. The machine did not pity me.”

“Machines do not respect you either.”

“Exactly. It was clean.”

Continuity Eight had contaminated that clean relationship. The machine's response now included business decisions, risk classifications and remote permission. What felt like bodily failure might be an architecture selected in a meeting.

Leena turned the recorder back on only after asking permission.

“What did you lose?”

“The belief that competence could make dependence disappear.”

That sentence never entered the pleadings. It shaped every one of them.

Mei Lin had her own account. During witness preparation she told Leena that Mira's discipline could become punishment directed at anyone who cared for her.

“She labels food by date, medicines by hour and people by whether they arrive on time,” Mei Lin said.

“Is she controlling?”

“She is frightened and organised. Sometimes those are the same from outside.”

Mira objected to the characterisation.

“See?” Mei Lin said. “She would like a correction procedure for her personality.”

Leena kept the remark out of evidence because affection was not impeachment. She kept it in her understanding of the client.

### The Group Learns Its Limits

The voluntary incident group met every Thursday after Celeste closed the canteen. They began with five rules: each person owned their story; no medical detail left without specific consent; disagreement remained in the minutes; urgent safety information went to the regulator before the press; and nobody promised an outcome.

The rules were tested immediately.

Celia wanted to publish a video of Lim's stairwell descent. It showed Jaya's prosthetic hand opening while she held the umbrella tether. The image could prove urgency. It also showed a neighbour in sleepwear, a child crying and the apartment numbers of residents who had not consented.

“Blur them,” Celia said.

“The original still leaves your phone,” Amina replied. “Who controls it after upload?”

“People need to see.”

Lim said the video made him look helpless. Celia accused him of protecting pride over safety. He accused her of making his worst night into content.

Mira wanted to impose a decision: preserve the original, provide it confidentially to the CSRA, release a redacted sequence if authorised. Instead she asked whether the group had appointed her to decide. It had not.

They voted after discussion. The original went to the CSRA under seal. A diagram and joint factual statement went public. Celia abstained and placed her objection in the minutes. Months later, she said the restraint had been right. Lim never asked her to say so.

Jaya tested another boundary. Her employer offered paid leave if she signed a statement that no device failure occurred on company premises. She needed the money. The group urged refusal until Amina reminded them of their first rule.

“Solidarity that commands her rent is another employer,” Amina said.

Leena negotiated different wording: the employer did not admit causation; Jaya did not waive claims; leave addressed an unresolved safety concern. It was less satisfying than a confession and more useful than poverty.

Celeste distrusted lawyers because an earlier wage complaint had consumed months and produced a letter she could not understand. Leena rewrote every advice note in plain language. Celeste still asked Amina to translate the legal meaning into ordinary consequences. Leena did not take offence. Trust was not owed because a professional had simplified a sentence.

Lim disliked SII terminology. He feared SII-3 would become a stamp on his identity and raise insurance costs. Imani explained that the Substrate Integrity Index assessed dependence and triggered protections. Lim replied that governments often created a protective category before institutions discovered profitable ways to use it.

They added another demand: SII data could be used only for specified governance purposes, with access logs, appeal rights and prohibition of adverse treatment unrelated to actual function. The demand later became part of the tribunal's privacy order.

The group disagreed about the word *eHuman*. Jaya liked it because it named integration without erasing humanity. Celeste thought employers would hear the prefix before the noun. Amina used both eHuman and eSapiens depending on context and refused purity tests.

“A legal vocabulary should give people tools,” she said. “If it becomes a password for belonging, it has failed.”

Mira recorded that sentence as a drafting principle.

Their meetings also contained life. Tara did homework at the corner table. Lim repaired a broken fan with tools he carried against everyone's advice. Celeste fed people who claimed not to be hungry. Amina's students tested accessible minutes. Celia designed icons and argued about colours. They celebrated when a help-desk ticket was acknowledged, mocked corporate phrases and sometimes discussed nothing related to the case.

One night, the canteen network failed. Every integrated device in the room displayed a warning. No function stopped because they had brought local controllers under Sen's temporary protocol.

Jaya raised a cup with her prosthetic hand. Amina spoke in her own voice. Lim stood without freezing. Celeste carried a pot of tea.

The network returned after ninety seconds. Nobody applauded it.

Leena Ortiz practised law above a bakery and below a dance school. During their first meeting, music travelled through the ceiling while warm air rose through the floor.

Her office was in Tanjong Pagar, close enough to the former port district that old maritime firms still occupied brass plaques in the stairwell. Leena had begun in a large commercial practice, defending medical-device manufacturers. She left after discovering that being correct about disclosure law did not make inadequate disclosure fair. She had once advised an insurer that funded an earlier Lattice generation. She disclosed the work, confirmed it created no continuing duty, and gave Mira the choice to leave.

Mira stayed. She preferred known complications.

Leena's conflict letter ran four pages. Mira read every word. Five years earlier, Leena had advised Northbridge Mutual on whether a Lattice service interruption qualified as device failure under an insurance policy. Her memorandum argued that temporary loss of enhanced performance was not necessarily loss of an Essential Function if a certified local state remained available.

“Vanguard cited you,” Mira said.

Leena did not pretend surprise. “They cited a sentence from a memorandum about an earlier architecture.”

“A sentence you wrote.”

“Yes.”

“Do you still believe it?”

“With the condition at the end: *if a certified local state remains available*.”

“That condition did not make the support script.”

“No.”

Mira considered leaving. A lawyer with no history would be easier to trust. A lawyer with Leena's history might understand exactly how a careful qualification became a corporate permission.

“You tell me everything they can use,” Mira said. “Not when they find it. Before.”

“The same rule applies to you.”

They shook hands without warmth.

Mira placed three binders on Leena's desk.

“Everything is indexed,” she said.

Leena opened the first binder, examined two pages, then closed it. “This is a great deal of information.”

“Four hundred and twelve incidents.”

“It is still not proof.”

Mira sat straighter. “The times correlate with network weakness.”

“Maybe. But we need admissible system records, an explanation of architecture, the update's regulatory status, evidence of notice, evidence of harm, and a causal bridge between them.”

“The pattern is obvious.”

“Obvious patterns lose cases every day.”

Leena asked who had authorized the diagnostic mode.

“No one.”

“Did you change any motor parameter?”

“No.”

“Can Vanguard prove that?”

“The logs can.”

“The logs you obtained by entering a restricted state?”

Mira disliked her immediately.

Leena continued. “Did you report the first failure?”

“As a routing incident.”

“As a medical incident?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I wanted to keep working.”

“That answer is human. It is not harmless. Vanguard will say your concealment deprived them of the opportunity to correct the problem.”

“Will you take the case?”

“I haven't decided whether there is a case.”

Mira placed the old certification beside the Continuity Eight notice. “This promises thirty days of independent operation. This requires daily authentication. Their support team says hardware did not change, as if classification measures the weight of metal in my body.”

Leena read both documents.

The music above them stopped.

“That,” she said, “may be the beginning of a case.”

She explained Section 3 of Lex-Cybernetica's proposed statutory framework. Classification turned on Essential Functions, remote dependence, software authority, reversibility, and consequences of failure. A material architectural change required reassessment. When indicators conflicted, the higher-risk tier governed safeguards but never civil status.

“They keep saying I haven't changed,” Mira said.

“That sentence is doing two jobs. It reassures you that you remain yourself, which was never legally in question. Then it suggests the system's risk has not changed, which is an engineering claim requiring evidence.”

For the first time in weeks, Mira felt the shape of the argument.

Leena agreed to seek an independent assessment and file a preservation notice preventing Vanguard from deleting update records. Her fee would be partly deferred, not forgiven.

“One more thing,” Leena said. “If you want me to present you as a faultless employee persecuted by a heartless corporation, find another lawyer.”

“I don't need to be faultless.”

“Good. Because you aren't.”

Mira almost smiled. “Neither are they.”

“That is what evidence is for.”

For the next month, evidence reorganised Mira's days. Before work she recorded baseline movements. At lunch she answered Leena's questions. Evenings went to document review. Weekends belonged to Sen's tests and Mei Lin's increasingly firm insistence that litigation did not excuse missed meals.

Leena made Mira construct two chronologies. The first contained what happened. The second contained when each person could be proved to know it. The distinction changed the case. Vanguard knew before deployment that prolonged disconnection impaired ordinary mobility. Mira knew only after Berth Nine that her own controller might be affected. Tomas knew her arm had failed but not why. Hale knew callers were dissatisfied before he understood the script avoided a material question.

“Responsibility follows control and knowledge,” Leena said. “Not omniscience we assign afterward.”

They rehearsed hostile questions. Why had Mira endangered Arjun? Why had she altered diagnostics? Was money her true motive? Did she resent users who benefited from the update?

Mira answered as if precision alone could protect her.

“Stop,” Leena said. “You sound like Vale.”

“You told me to be careful.”

“Careful is not bloodless. A judge can hear when a witness has edited herself into a policy document.”

Mira tried again. “I was afraid that reporting would cost my berth.”

“Did that justify silence?”

“No.”

“Then say no. Explanation is not acquittal.”

At the bakery below, Leena bought coffee and admitted she had once taught corporate witnesses the same technique. “Answer the narrow question truthfully. Never volunteer the architecture of the decision.”

“And now?”

“Now I ask courts to compel the architecture.”

Neither called that redemption.

Leena explained the forum. Under the story's 2038 Cyber-Substrate Governance Act, Singapore had adopted core Lex-Cybernetica safeguards and established the Cyber-Jurisprudence Division as a specialist list within the General Division of the High Court. The CSRA made technical classifications and regulatory orders. The CJD reviewed legality, procedural fairness, evidence and proportionality; connected compensation questions could be joined where the statute allowed an effective remedy.

She placed [Lex-Cybernetica](https://duck-14.gitbook.io/lex-cybernetica) beside Mira's logs. Its vocabulary separated problems corporations preferred to merge: **Material Control**, **Essential Function**, **Cognitive Telemetry**, **Analog Fallback State**, **Substrate Preservation Order**, and **Substrate Parity**.

Substrate Parity protected both an eHuman such as Mira and a recognized eSapient without pretending that their architectures were identical. Equality did not require technical sameness. It required that substrate never become permission for domination.

“It is not a magical court,” Leena said. “It cannot repair engineering by declaring a principle. We need an order someone can test.”

Mira wrote that down.

Leena's first preservation request was deliberately narrow: the certified architecture, update decision record, user disclosures, support guidance, incident categories and Mira's device logs. A demand for every Continuity Eight record would invite delay and a proportionality objection. A narrow request forced Vanguard either to preserve the decisive material or identify why it was not decisive.

“What if they delete something before the order?” Mira asked.

“The notice creates duties now. Destruction after notice can support sanctions and evidentiary consequences. But courts do not conjure missing proof merely because we suspect it once existed.”

“So we trust them.”

“No. We create consequences for untrustworthiness.”

That difference became Leena's first lesson in law as engineering. A rule was useful only if its breach produced a response that could still protect someone.

At home, Mira tried to explain the case to Mei Lin without using SII terminology.

“They moved part of the machine outside me,” she said.

“The part is where?”

“In their computers.”

“Put it back.”

“That is more or less the case.”

Mei Lin nodded. “Your lawyer charges by the hour to say *put it back*?”

“She charges to make them answer.”

“Then feed her. Hungry people ask short questions.”

The next morning Mei Lin delivered two containers of food to Leena's office. It was the first alliance in the case that Mira had not organised.

## Chapter Six — The Analog Test

Dr. Nadiya Sen refused to call the procedure a disconnection test.

“Disconnection describes what we do to the network,” she said. “Analog fallback describes what the system must do for you.”

The assessment room contained a padded walkway, handrails, local medical controls, and no connection to Vanguard. A CSRA observer verified the isolation seal. Mira wore a support harness she regarded as insulting until Sen asked whether pride could prevent a fractured wrist.

Mira allowed the harness.

At full connection, she crossed the walkway in twelve seconds. Her left stride was shorter than her right but stable.

Sen disabled the external link.

For three seconds, nothing changed. Then Mira felt the implant draw inward. Assistance at her hip decreased. Fine control left her fingers. A warning appeared:

`NETWORK UNAVAILABLE. SAFE DEGRADED STATE ACTIVE.`

“Walk when ready,” Sen said.

Mira ordered her left foot forward. It moved six centimetres and stopped. She shifted her weight. The knee trembled. The harness tightened.

“Again,” she said.

On the third attempt she completed one step. On the seventh she reached the midpoint. Sweat ran beneath the sensors at her neck.

“We have enough,” Sen said.

“The full distance.”

“This is not a test of character.”

“The full distance.”

Sen let her continue.

Each step required negotiation among intention, residual biological signal and the controller's reduced local model. Mira could feel the instant when movement stopped being anticipated and became correction. Her hip shifted after imbalance instead of before it. Her handrail grip tightened too late. The harness caught her twice without lifting her fully.

Observers watched numbers. Mira watched the exit sign at the end of the room. Regulations often referred to preserving life, preventing collapse and maintaining emergency stability. She imagined waking in her flat during a network failure, smelling smoke from another unit and discovering that emergency stability meant remaining safely unable to reach the stairs.

“Add evacuation,” she said when the test ended.

Sen looked up from the trace.

“To the functional criteria. Not only standing and walking in a clinic. Reaching an exit, operating a door, calling for assistance.”

The CSRA observer objected that evacuation standards belonged to building safety.

“My leg does not know which regulator owns the corridor,” Mira said.

Sen added a recommendation for scenario-based testing across foreseeable ordinary needs. It later became one of the most cited parts of her report.

Mira crossed in four minutes and nineteen seconds. At the end, she tried to lift an empty cup with her left hand. Her fingers closed too slowly and pushed it from the table.

The cup rolled in a circle on the floor.

Sen repeated the task with an empty cup, a bottle, a keycard and an emergency door handle. Connected, Mira completed all four. With fresh cache she managed three. With aged cache she dropped the card and could not rotate the handle far enough to release its latch.

“The handle meets accessibility standards,” the observer said.

“For the force my certified system used to provide,” Mira answered.

The test exposed a seam. Building accessibility assumed the device met specification. Device certification assumed the environment met accessibility standards. Each system passed alone. Together, during outage, Mira remained behind the door.

Sen designed an integrated evacuation protocol. Morrow agreed after adding safeguards against fatigue bias. Their cooperation irritated both legal teams and improved the evidence.

The lights dimmed.

Sen looked toward the isolation console. A network outside the sealed room had failed, triggering the building's emergency supply. The motor-assist recovery controller was drawing from a battery whose status display showed forty-one minutes.

“Restore her link,” the CSRA observer said.

“External service is unavailable,” the technician replied.

For the first time, the test ceased to be entirely controlled.

Sen lowered Mira into a chair and connected a portable local stimulator. It could support posture, not walking. The building announced a routing fault and asked occupants to remain where they were.

Mira imagined thousands of Lattice users receiving the same polite message. “How many controllers authenticate through this region?”

No one answered.

She called Mei Lin. Her mother's hearing support was local and unaffected, but she heard the tension in Mira's breathing.

“Use the chair,” Mei Lin said.

“What chair?”

“Whichever one you are pretending not to need.”

Twenty-eight minutes later, service returned. No one was injured, but Sen amended her report: an emergency mode requiring clinical equipment and stationary supervision could not reasonably be described as independent operation.

Her amendment included a sentence Mira repeated throughout the case: **A safe mode must be safe for a life, not merely stable for a machine.** It became the practical test of an **Analog Fallback State (AFS)**.

Vanguard's expert, Dr. Rafiq Morrow, requested a second test. He argued that Sen's isolation protocol disabled both external prediction and local cache refresh, a combination more severe than an ordinary outage. Sen agreed that the distinction deserved testing.

Mira objected. “The first test answered the question.”

“It answered one question,” Sen said.

“You saw me.”

“I did. I also saw an unexpected building fault. If we fail to separate variables, Vanguard will be right to challenge the result.”

The second test preserved the most recent prediction cache while blocking ongoing external computation. Mira crossed the walkway in fifty-eight seconds—slow, unstable, but far better than four minutes. Morrow called that evidence of an adequate fallback.

Then Sen repeated the test after allowing the cache to age for the maximum interval permitted by Vanguard's own update schedule. Mira's time rose to three minutes and forty-seven seconds, and fine-motor function again failed.

The competing results complicated the case. The fallback worked reasonably for a short interruption soon after synchronization. It deteriorated as cached predictions aged. Vanguard had tested the first condition and advertised it as if it covered the second.

“They did not invent the result,” Leena said afterward.

“They selected the hour in which it was true.”

“That may be more important.”

Mira wanted a clean lie. Instead she received a boundary hidden inside an average.

“Am I SII-3?” Mira asked.

Sen did not offer comfort in place of precision. “Classification belongs to the CSRA. My opinion is that Continuity Eight introduced recurring network dependence and material external authority over motor mediation. Those are SII-3 indicators.”

“But I remained conscious. I could move.”

“SII-3 does not mean helpless. It does not mean less human. It means the system requires safeguards proportionate to its architecture.”

“Vanguard says the mode is safe.”

“Safe from catastrophic collapse may not be safe for ordinary life. Regulation 3.1 requires a tested safe local state for an integration mediating an Essential Function. Regulation 3.2 requires plain disclosure of offline duration and degraded functions. You were told neither that walking could take twenty times longer nor that fine motor assistance would substantially disappear.”

Sen restored the link. Strength returned to Mira's leg before relief had time to become gratitude.

She hated the network for returning what it had taught her to expect.

Outside, Leena asked how she felt.

“Measured.”

“The index measures the system.”

“I know what the law says.”

“Knowing and feeling are different jurisdictions.”

Mira looked back at the sealed room. “For four minutes in there, I thought reclassification meant admitting they were right about me.”

“Who?”

“Everyone who thinks dependence is failure.”

Leena handed her the preliminary report. “Then perhaps this case is about making the distinction impossible to ignore.”

### The Archive of Warnings

Sen did not allow the analog test to become a spectacle. No reporter entered the laboratory. No livestream carried Mira's tremor to strangers. The only observers were the parties, two independent engineers, a CSRA recorder and, at Mira's request, one representative chosen by the voluntary incident group. They chose Amina because she noticed every euphemism.

While the engineers sealed the equipment, Leena opened a folder marked **HISTORICAL CONTEXT — NOT PROOF OF CAUSATION**.

“Why that warning?” Mira asked.

“Because analogies become dishonest when they pretend to be identities.” Leena set out two old regulatory notices. “These events do not prove Vanguard is liable. They prove that connected medical devices have presented cybersecurity and continuity risks before, and that regulators have required corrective action before catastrophe.”

The first record came from the United States Food and Drug Administration. In 2017, firmware had been released for certain implantable cardiac pacemakers to reduce the risk of unauthorised access through radio-frequency communications. The second concerned a 2019 recall of specified MiniMed insulin pumps whose wireless vulnerabilities could, under particular conditions, permit an unauthorised person to change settings and affect insulin delivery.

Mira read the dates twice. “People knew this problem existed.”

“People knew versions of it existed,” Sen corrected. “A pacemaker is not your controller. An insulin pump is not Jaya's hand. Cybersecurity is not identical to commercial authentication. But the history defeats one lazy story: that network dependence inside a medical or bodily system arrived without warning.”

Amina typed. Her restored voice said, “Were patients told to disconnect?”

“Sometimes the safer course was a firmware update,” Sen said. “Sometimes replacement or disabling features carried its own clinical risk. The lesson is not ‘connection bad.’ It is that safety decisions must be specific, disclosed and supported.”

Leena placed another page beside the notices. It contained a sentence from Frederick Douglass's 1857 address: “Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will.”

“Public domain,” she said when Mira looked at her. “And accurate enough to survive fashion.”

“A demand is not evidence,” Mira said.

“No. But evidence without a demand can remain in a drawer.”

Amina entered the sentence into the group's minutes, followed immediately by Mira's qualification. That became their habit: proposition and limit, courage and discipline.

The laboratory door unlocked. Pell entered carrying a local controller in a transparent case. He looked as though he had not slept.

“This is the pre-update Analog Fallback State,” he said. “It contains no active wide-area interface. Its safety envelope is fixed to the clinically validated baseline.”

“And its limitations?” Sen asked.

“Reduced prediction horizon. No fleet learning. Manual calibration after six hours. Possible fatigue under sustained precision work.”

“Then why was it removed from ordinary issue?”

Pell glanced at Vanguard's counsel. “That is outside the test protocol.”

Amina's device said, “Everything important is outside somebody's protocol.”

The recorder asked the parties to refrain from commentary. Amina requested that her sentence remain in the transcript. Vanguard objected. The recorder preserved both sentence and objection.

Mira lay beneath the diagnostic frame while Sen switched the Lattice from Continuity Eight to the sealed controller. The first sensation was absence. The constant minute corrections stopped. Her left leg felt heavier, older, and entirely hers to command. She lifted her foot, held it for five seconds and lowered it within the marked square.

“Again,” Sen said.

Twenty repetitions produced no latency spike. Forty produced fatigue. At sixty-three, Mira's ankle shook and missed the square by two centimetres. Vanguard's expert recorded the miss with visible relief.

“Local control is inferior,” he said.

“Inferior at what?” Sen asked.

“Precision stability.”

“After sixty-three repetitions. What was the maximum safe continuous repetition disclosed in the original clinical instructions?”

The expert consulted his tablet. “Fifty.”

“So the local system remained within its stated envelope and warned at fifty-one. Continuity Eight extended performance but introduced a remote failure mode. Those are two truths, not one ranking.”

Mira sat up, sweating. For years she had treated every limit as an obstacle to conceal. The fixed controller announced its limit without shame. The networked system had promised continuity while hiding the conditions under which continuity vanished.

The group then tested Jaya's hand under three network transitions. On the local baseline, the grip held and haptic resolution declined gradually. Under Continuity Eight, the licence check failed during the transition from the clinic network to the isolated test cell. The fingers opened.

Jaya had insisted on holding something real. She chose Tara's steel lunch box, empty except for a folded note from her daughter: *Come home with your hand, Ma.*

The box fell six centimetres into Sen's padded cradle.

Jaya did not cry. Pell did.

He turned away before Vanguard's counsel could see, but Mira saw. Later, in the corridor, Pell explained that the hand-release response came from a security rule: unverified control instructions could not remain latched because a malicious command might maintain a dangerous grip.

“The engineers were solving a real hazard,” he said. “If a compromised system clenches around a person's throat or a machine lever, fail-open can save a life.”

“And if it opens above boiling water?” Jaya asked.

“It can destroy one.”

“So which is it?”

“It should depend on context. The device knows force, orientation and object temperature. We proposed contextual hold-safe mode.”

“Where is it?”

“Deferred.”

“Because?”

Pell did not answer. That answer appeared months later in the missing appendix.

Lim's gait regulator created a different finding. In local mode, he walked slowly with a cane. During cloud authentication failure, Continuity Eight froze his right knee at the beginning of a step. A safety harness caught him.

“Again,” Lim said.

Sen refused. “The result is established.”

“An engineer repeats.”

“A patient does not owe us a fall to improve our confidence interval.”

Lim studied her, then nodded. “Put that in the report.”

She did.

Celeste's tremor system was tested with cold vessels before any heated object. Local mode permitted a visible tremor but maintained the expected direction. Connected mode compensated more smoothly until simulated congestion delayed a correction, after which the sleeve over-corrected by nine degrees.

“Nine degrees is within acceptable variance,” Vanguard's expert said.

Celeste placed the empty saucepan on the bench. “At the handle, perhaps. At the rim, it crosses a child's face.”

The room became quiet.

Sen measured the arc from the rim rather than the handle. The potential displacement was twenty-three centimetres. She added both figures to the report.

Amina underwent the final test. Vanguard argued that substitution with a generic voice caused no physical impairment and ensured communication continued. Amina asked the recorder to play two phrases without labels. The first used her personal model: “Evacuate through the west stair.” The second used the generic voice saying the same words.

Mira heard information in both. In the first she also heard Amina: the clipped consonant before a command, the small upward turn that made urgency precise rather than panicked. Her students and family recognised those cues. They were not decorative.

“The service specification guarantees intelligibility, not identity,” Vanguard's counsel said.

Amina typed for a long time.

“That sentence is your whole case,” her device finally said. “You took identity because your specification did not count it.”

Leena did not interrupt. She knew when a lawyer's improvement would make a truth smaller.

At the end of the session, the recorder sealed six copies of the data. One went to each party, one to the CSRA, one to Sen's institutional archive and one to a trustee selected by the incident group. Mira insisted on the last copy because corporate records could disappear and personal copies could be dismissed as altered.

Outside, rain hammered the sheltered walkway. Jaya opened an umbrella with her prosthetic hand. It held.

“Local mode?” Mira asked.

“No. Luck.”

They walked to the station together, careful not to confuse one with the other.

### The Night at Block 418

Lim's housing block lost external connectivity six weeks before the hearing. The cause was ordinary: excavation equipment severed two fibre routes during drainage work. Building power remained. Lifts halted because their remote safety service could not reconcile access tokens. Residents were told to remain in place until technicians arrived.

At 2:13 a.m., smoke sensors detected an electrical fire in a ground-floor utility cupboard. Fire doors closed, alarms sounded and the public-address system instructed residents to use the stairs. The fire was contained within the cupboard, but no one on the upper floors could know that.

Lim lived on the nineteenth floor.

His local gait controller was in a clinic locker awaiting authorisation for home issue. Continuity Eight had entered safe immobility when the network failed. Celia woke to the alarm and found her grandfather seated on the edge of his bed, striking the regulator with the heel of his hand.

“Do not hit a control system,” she said, breathless.

“Then give me one that listens.”

She tried the emergency gait command. The device displayed **REMOTE VALIDATION UNAVAILABLE — MAINTAIN STABLE POSITION**.

Neighbours knocked. An evacuation marshal shouted that the east stair was clear. Lim ordered Celia to leave without him. She refused. He ordered her again, using the voice that had once stopped apprentices from reaching into live machinery.

“I will not have two people trapped because one machine has manners,” he said.

Celia opened the analog procedure Sen had insisted every group member keep on paper. The instructions did not unlock the gait implant; only a clinician-issued key could do that. But they described a manual support pattern using a cane, belt and second person's shoulder. It was designed for three steps to safety, not nineteen floors.

At the seventeenth floor, Lim's left leg began to tremble. At the fifteenth, the stairwell filled with residents moving around them. Some complained. Most helped. A delivery rider named Faiz carried their water and shouted their position down the stairwell. An elderly neighbour held each fire door. A boy counted landings because Lim had lost track.

At the twelfth floor, the regulator attempted to reconnect and locked both knees during a token exchange. Lim folded forward. Celia braced beneath his arm; her shoes slipped on the stair edge.

Jaya caught the back of Celia's jacket.

She had come from the adjacent block after seeing the incident-group alert. Her prosthetic hand closed on the heavy fabric. The network transition in the stairwell triggered its licence check.

The fingers began to release.

Jaya drove her elbow through the umbrella loop she wore around her wrist, wrapped the loop twice around Celia's jacket and used her natural hand to lock the strap. The prosthesis opened completely. The improvised tether held.

For three seconds, Lim, Celia and Jaya balanced above a flight of concrete steps, their combined weight carried by a cheap umbrella strap and the rail.

Then Faiz climbed back, planted himself below Lim and pushed him upright. The boy stopped counting. Everyone in the stairwell became silent.

“Eleven floors,” Lim said.

“Twelve,” the boy whispered.

“Good. You are still employed.”

The boy laughed first. The laughter moved down the stairwell like a signal more useful than the failed network.

Firefighters met them at the ninth floor with an evacuation chair. Lim objected that others needed it more. The officer told him this was not a committee. They strapped him in and carried him to the ground.

Outside, rain had begun. The utility fire was already extinguished. Celia sat on the kerb, shaking so hard she could not unlock her phone. Lim reached for her hand. His regulator reconnected at that moment and straightened his leg abruptly, kicking the evacuation chair's footrest.

“Excellent timing,” he said.

Celia laughed and sobbed against his shoulder. Jaya stood nearby staring at her open prosthetic fingers.

The next morning, a broadcaster called the descent miraculous. Lim corrected the word during a live interview.

“It was not a miracle,” he said. “It was neighbours, paper instructions, a rider who came back up, a child who counted correctly, and an umbrella manufacturer with a generous safety margin. If you call preparation a miracle, officials are free to remain surprised.”

The clip travelled across Singapore. Vanguard issued a statement that the implant had behaved according to its safety design and that building connectivity fell outside its control.

Leena advised Lim that a public statement might complicate his claim. He replied that a claim which required him to lie about nineteen floors was not worth making.

The CSRA opened an emergency inquiry. Within seventy-two hours, it ordered temporary analog controllers for all mobility users in buildings whose evacuation depended on stairs. Vanguard appealed the breadth of the order and lost.

Mira arrived at Lim's flat with a certified controller and found half the block in his kitchen. The boy from the stairwell was repairing the umbrella with tape. Faiz had brought breakfast. Jaya's daughter Tara was teaching Celia how to braid hair one-handed so she could help Jaya when the prosthesis failed.

Lim connected the local controller. He stood. The first step was slow; the second shook; the third held.

No music played. No official announced a victory. Yet when he reached the corridor under his own command, neighbours began clapping from open doorways.

Mira watched Jaya close her hand around Tara's fingers. The grip remained stable.

For one morning, joy did not feel like denial. It felt like evidence of what redundancy protected: not merely survival, but the ordinary return from danger into breakfast, teasing and a child's impatient lesson.

## Chapter Seven — Acceptable Variance

Vanguard's settlement offer arrived in a box designed for expensive gifts.

The box was hand-delivered rather than sent through the case portal. Its paper copy bore a watermark and tabs at every signature. Presentation suggested conclusion before negotiation began.

Leena required Mira to wait twenty-four hours. “Urgency belongs to them. Understanding belongs to you.”

Mira spent the evening calculating. The sum paid rehabilitation debt, restored Mei Lin's renovation money and replaced years of savings. The support guarantee mattered more: if Vanguard withdrew her controller generation, she would not migrate alone.

At three in the morning she opened the confidentiality clause. It allowed disclosure to clinicians and family if they accepted secrecy. It did not allow her to warn another user that ordinary mobility might disappear in a prolonged outage.

The clause did not ask her to lie. It purchased the conditions under which truth could be spoken.

It proposed full restoration of Mira's lost allowance, payment of legal and medical expenses, a new implant controller, and five years of premium support. In return, she would withdraw the classification petition, keep the terms confidential, and acknowledge that her response to Continuity Eight represented individual physiological variance.

The amount would clear every debt created by her accident.

The offer also contained a controller unavailable through ordinary clinical procurement. Vanguard would restore a full local prediction model for Mira alone and guarantee parts for five years. It was, technically, much of what she wanted.

Leena separated the pages into three piles: compensation, medical support and releases. The release extended beyond Mira's individual claims. It required her to withdraw the classification petition, decline participation in related proceedings and return copies of internal materials produced voluntarily before formal disclosure.

“Can they ask for that?” Mira said.

“They can ask. The court would examine enforceability if disputed. We decide before signing.”

Mira carried the offer to Mei Lin's flat. The accessibility renovation had begun; furniture was covered in plastic and the bathroom doorway stood without a frame. Dust settled on everything.

“This pays for the machine you need?” Mei Lin asked.

“Yes.”

“And you stop?”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you asking me?”

Mira said she wanted advice.

“No. You want permission for whichever choice makes you feel guilty.”

They ate takeaway among the covered furniture. Mei Lin described years working payroll for a shipping agency, where every exception required a manager's signature and every manager asked whether the exception could become precedent.

“Precedent,” she said, “was the word for helping no one twice.”

She did not tell Mira whether to sign.

“It is a serious offer,” Leena said.

“You mean I should take it.”

“I mean refusing it must also be serious.”

Mira had imagined lawyers as engines that converted indignation into victory. Leena kept returning decisions to their owner.

Leena also made her consider losing. The court might order reassessment and deny most damages. Public identification could make employers cautious. There was no moral prize guaranteed for rejecting money.

“People romanticise plaintiffs after someone else pays their rent,” Leena said.

Mira asked what she would do.

“A lawyer who answers steals the client's decision.”

Mira walked through Tuas View, past warehouses, food stalls and businesses serving the port's hidden human infrastructure. From a distance the automated terminal looked self-contained. Up close it depended on cleaners, cooks, repairers, inspectors and people carrying parts between systems that did not share procurement databases.

Efficiency had always rested on someone absorbing the mismatch.

At the terminal, Mira spent her days reconciling container labels. Tomas had become acting systems supervisor. The promotion gave him a glass cubicle and the authority to approve the reports they once wrote together.

“Take the money,” he told her quietly. “You get fixed. They get to keep pretending. That is already what everyone does.”

“Everyone?”

Tomas showed her a private queue of adaptation reports. A crane operator whose augmented wrist froze during authentication. A dispatcher whose visual overlay failed in the western stack. Two drivers reporting leg-assist delays after Continuity Eight.

Each case had been coded separately. None mentioned network dependence.

“How long have you known?” Mira asked.

“A week.”

“And you said nothing.”

“You said nothing first.”

The truth landed cleanly.

That evening Mira visited Imani Sayeed, whose ankle complaint she had once helped turn into a procedural absence. Imani now repaired domestic lift systems from a shop in Pioneer, not far from the port but outside its security perimeter. She had built a steady business from work Vanguard once treated as beneath her. She moved carefully, not weakly, using a simpler locally controlled ankle.

“I came to apologize,” Mira said.

“Three years late.”

“Yes.”

Imani continued sorting screws. “Are you apologizing because you understand, or because you need me for your case?”

“Both.”

Imani laughed once. “At least your lawyer trained you.”

She would not testify. Her records were incomplete, and she wanted no further relationship with Vanguard. But before Mira left, she said, “They survive because every problem is private. Your settlement has confidentiality because isolation is one of the services they're buying.”

Three days later Imani called back. She still would not become a damages witness. She would help affected workers submit their own records directly to the CSRA.

“Not for you,” she said. “For the next person you might otherwise call procedure.”

Her decision transformed four names on Mira's wall into nine sworn incident statements. It also prevented Vanguard from treating Mira's logs as the only evidence of a common pattern.

Imani organised the workers at a community room in Jurong West. She placed Mira at the side of the table rather than the front.

“This is not your support group,” she said.

The nine accounts did not align neatly. Two users liked Continuity Eight and feared that Mira's case would force Vanguard to withdraw it. One had experienced only display errors. Another admitted missing calibration appointments. A crane planner named Siti Rahman said network prediction had reduced the spasms that once ended her shifts early.

“If they put everything local again,” Siti told Mira, “I lose what works.”

“I am asking for a fallback and correct classification.”

“Your public statement says the update took control.”

“It took control from me.”

“Reporters do not keep the last two words.”

Imani let the discomfort continue. Later she told Mira, “A collective case is not nine copies of your case. If the law protects autonomy, it must protect her choice to remain connected too.”

The panel included eHumans across SII-1, SII-2 and SII-3 profiles, with an eSapient observer appointed under the CSRA's substrate-representation rules.

“Substrate Parity does not erase asymmetry,” the observer said. “Vanguard controls infrastructure Mira requires. Mira does not control infrastructure Vanguard requires. Rights must notice that direction.”

Mira wrote a shorter phrase in her notebook: **A threshold is not a throne.** No SII tier conferred authority over another.

The petition was revised. It did not demand universal rollback. It sought reassessment, meaningful disclosure, tested local safety and individual choice where technically feasible. For users whose clinical benefit required continuous service, it sought redundancy and continuity guarantees rather than forced disconnection.

Mira disliked the added complexity until she recognised it as the point.

Mira returned home and read the offer again.

She wanted the controller. She wanted the debt gone. She wanted to stop being observed by people who had never needed permission to lift a cup.

She called Leena.

“Reject it.”

“Are you certain?”

“No.”

“That is not the same as uninformed.”

“Reject it anyway.”

Two days later, the CSRA accepted Mira's petition for a contested classification review.

Vanguard's public statement described her as a valued employee experiencing an uncommon adaptation difficulty.

Mira pinned the statement above her desk.

Under *uncommon*, she wrote the names of four other workers.

Public reaction divided along a sentence no one in the case had actually used: *Should one employee stop technology that helps twelve thousand?* Vanguard never placed the words in an advertisement. Friendly commentators discovered them without instruction.

Mira's employee profile circulated beside footage of the service-link event. Some viewers called her brave. Others asked why she had endangered a trainee after concealing a malfunction. Both descriptions were accurate enough to hurt.

Mei Lin read the comments until Mira took away her tablet.

“You said it was about the machine,” her mother said. “They are talking about your character.”

“That is how you avoid talking about the machine.”

“Are the things about Arjun true?”

Mira sat beside her. “Yes.”

“All of them?”

“I knew about a possible earlier failure. I went onto the link. No one was hurt.”

“That last sentence is luck, not defence.”

Mei Lin walked to the kitchen. She did not ask Mira to leave, but she did not pack food for her either.

For eleven days they communicated only through short messages about medicine and appointments. Mira had expected litigation to cost money and privacy. She had not understood that it would force every relationship to decide whether truth was a condition of loyalty.

## Chapter Eight — The Missing Appendix

### The Price of a Private Ending

The settlement deadline was noon on Monday. At 11:20, Mira sat in Leena's office with the signature page before her and Mei Lin's care estimate beside it.

Vanguard had improved the offer. It would fund a certified controller for life, restore lost employment benefits and pay enough to secure Mei Lin's home support. It would also classify Mira's device as SII-3 for internal care purposes. In return, Mira would withdraw the public reclassification claim, keep the architecture confidential and agree that no tribunal had determined wrongdoing.

The offer solved nearly everything that had brought her to Leena.

It solved nothing for Jaya, Amina, Lim or Celeste.

“The group claims do not legally belong to you,” Leena said. “They can continue without you.”

“Practically?”

“Your test evidence is central. Vanguard would seek to seal it under the settlement. We would argue that regulatory copies remain. There is risk.”

“How much?”

“Anyone who gives you a percentage is selling comfort.”

Mei Lin had refused to attend. She said her need should not sit in the room pretending to be advice.

Mira called each group member separately. Jaya told her to take the money if she needed it. Amina said the decision was Mira's and then remained silent so long the call felt like judgment. Lim said a structure depending on one claimant deserved to fall. Celeste asked whether Mei Lin was safe alone.

At 11:47, Vale called directly. Leena placed him on speaker and recorded with consent.

“This is not an attempt to pressure you,” Vale said.

“Then why now?” Mira asked.

“Because counsel cannot say what I want to say. Daniel died last year.”

Mira knew his brother had deteriorated. She had not known he died.

“His local controller was six versions behind,” Vale continued. “The insurer approved a connected upgrade after eighteen months. It arrived four weeks after his death. When people demand redundant hardware, bespoke review and individual certification, I hear delay measured in bodies.”

Mira let the sentence stand before answering. “Amina's father died while her own voice asked a server for permission. She does not claim the outage killed him. She claims delay can contain a life even when it does not end one.”

“I know her evidence.”

“Do you?”

“Do not make grief a credential, Mira. We both have it.”

The rebuke was deserved. She had begun to deploy Amina's loss as moral leverage, exactly what Amina prohibited.

Vale said the settlement could become a model: guaranteed support for users who demonstrated material failure, without imposing costly fallback on everyone.

“People must first fail convincingly,” Mira said.

“People must show a remedy is needed.”

“Your architecture created the dependence before asking whether the remedy was needed.”

“And improved tens of thousands of lives.”

“Both are true.”

Vale became quiet. “Then why can we not settle on both?”

“Because your agreement requires one truth to remain private.”

At 11:56, Mira signed the rejection. Her hand trembled halfway through the surname. Leena witnessed it without comment and transmitted the page.

At 12:03, the care agency informed Mira it could not hold Mei Lin's preferred schedule without a deposit. The timing was accidental and felt punitive.

Mira went home expecting anger. Mei Lin was at the kitchen table repairing a torn blouse.

“I refused,” Mira said.

“I heard.”

“How?”

“You walk differently when you have spent money you do not possess.”

Mira explained the care schedule. Mei Lin continued sewing.

“When your father lost the shop,” she said, “he hid the bank letters because he thought worry was his duty. We discovered debt after choices were gone. Protection without information is vanity.”

She handed Mira the care estimate. Together they reduced hours, asked relatives for specific help and applied for a subsidy. The plan was less safe than the settlement would have funded, but it was chosen with both lives visible.

That night Mei Lin woke disoriented and called Mira by her sister's name. The episode lasted seven minutes. Mira sat beside her until memory returned.

“Do not put this in your case,” Mei Lin whispered.

“Why would I?”

“Because everything sad has become evidence.”

Mira turned off the bedside recorder she used for medication timing. For once, no log preserved the minute. She held Mei Lin's hand until dawn and accepted that not every truth needed to become a public instrument.

### A Narrow Escape at the Causeway

The incident that changed public opinion did not involve a port or a courtroom. It occurred on a commuter bus approaching the Woodlands checkpoint during evening rain.

Jaya and Tara were returning from a clinic calibration. The bus carried thirty-two passengers, including two eHumans using mobility systems and an eSapiens courier wearing an external powered brace. Traffic stopped beneath a flyover after flood sensors closed the road ahead.

The bus network switched between Singapore carriers near the checkpoint. Jaya's prosthetic hand entered verification mode. At the same moment, the bus driver suffered a sudden cardiac event and slumped over the wheel.

The vehicle's lane automation maintained direction but did not brake because the driver-assistance system interpreted his hand on the wheel as supervision. Ahead, traffic was stationary.

A passenger shouted. Tara saw the driver's foot remain on the accelerator. She unfastened her belt and ran forward before Jaya could stop her.

The bus was moving at thirty-eight kilometres per hour.

Jaya reached for the emergency rail. Her prosthesis opened. She caught the rail with her natural hand, swung into the aisle and fell against a seat. Tara reached the driver but could not move his leg.

The eSapiens courier, Harish Bose, detached his powered brace from delivery mode. Its contract prohibited passenger assistance because extra load could damage the frame. A warning said unauthorised use might void coverage.

He ignored it.

Harish locked the brace, pushed through the aisle and lifted the driver's knee from the accelerator. Another passenger pulled the red emergency lever. The lever requested driver confirmation on the dashboard.

“Manual brake!” someone shouted.

The bus model had one, a recessed pedal beside the driver's seat intended for depot towing. Nobody could find it. Tara remembered the tactile emergency diagram from a school accessibility lesson. She dropped to the floor, followed raised markings beneath the dashboard and found the pedal.

It required more force than a child could apply.

Jaya crawled forward. Her artificial hand was still open. She placed her shoulder over Tara's foot. Harish braced both of them with the exoskeleton. Together they pressed.

The bus stopped less than a metre from the vehicle ahead.

For several seconds, rain struck the roof and no one spoke. Then the lane automation announced: **UNSCHEDULED STOP. DRIVER INPUT REQUIRED.**

Harish laughed so hard he had to sit down.

Passengers called emergency services. A nurse on the bus began resuscitation. The driver reached hospital alive and underwent treatment. Doctors later said prompt assistance materially improved his chance of recovery.

The courier's brace logged structural overload and locked. Harish could walk without it, but carrying parcels was his livelihood. His employer suspended him for unauthorised use pending investigation.

Jaya's first instinct was to make the story about the failed hand. Tara refused.

“The hand failed,” she said. “The bus failed. The red lever failed. Harish's rule failed. People did not.”

The sentence appeared in news reports. Commentators called Tara heroic. She cried that night because the driver's face returned whenever she closed her eyes. Jaya found her sitting on the bathroom floor at 3 a.m.

“Heroes are not afraid after,” Tara said.

“Who told you?”

“Stories.”

“Stories skip the bathroom.”

Jaya sat beside her. The prosthetic hand had reconnected and could now close perfectly. Tara would not let it touch her until morning.

Harish's employer reversed the suspension after public criticism, but the brace manufacturer denied warranty coverage. Leena referred him to a consumer-law clinic and refused to absorb his claim into Mira's case merely because it was useful. His brace was external, his SII different, and the contract issue distinct. Lex-Cybernetica could inform analysis without swallowing established labour, disability and product law.

The manufacturer eventually repaired the brace and rewrote the emergency-use exception. Harish asked that Tara press the activation control when he collected it.

“Why me?” she asked.

“Because you read instructions nobody else could find.”

The recovered bus driver, Mr Tan, attended. He remembered nothing between the rain and the hospital. He thanked each person, then apologised for causing trouble.

“You became ill,” Jaya said. “That is not misconduct.”

Mira heard about the reunion from Leena. She resisted using it publicly until Jaya asked the group to include a careful account in its submission. The account identified separate failures and successes. It did not claim Continuity Eight caused the driver's illness or the bus design. It showed why fallback must be discoverable, physically usable and compatible with people whose bodies and devices differ.

Vanguard tried to distinguish the incident. The company was correct: it did not supply the bus system or Harish's brace. The distinction did not weaken the principle. It prevented the principle from becoming propaganda.

### The Historical Table

To prepare the policy submission, Leena built a historical table with three columns: event, verified lesson and prohibited inference.

The 2017 United States pacemaker firmware action demonstrated that regulators had addressed cybersecurity risk in implantable cardiac devices through corrective firmware. It did not prove all networked implants were unsafe. The 2019 MiniMed pump recall demonstrated that wireless vulnerabilities in a medical device could create a risk of unauthorised changes to therapy. It did not prove Vanguard's authentication design was malicious or technically identical.

Mira wanted older industrial examples. Lim brought reports about machinery interlocks, aviation checklists and maritime disasters. Leena excluded any they could not source precisely. Famous tragedies carried emotional force that could overwhelm technical difference.

“History is not a costume we put on the present,” she said. “Use it to show that a category of risk was foreseeable, then prove this case with this case.”

Amina added a fourth column: whose experience had been omitted at the time. Patients, operators, nurses, maintenance workers, families and people unable to access warnings often knew different parts of a system. Technical histories improved when those observations entered formal records instead of remaining anecdotes.

Lim quoted Frederick Douglass: “Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will.”

Jaya asked who had said it and in what context. Lim admitted he knew the sentence from a poster, not the 1857 address. They found the archival text and read the surrounding argument. Douglass was speaking about struggle, power and the necessity of demand in the campaign against slavery and oppression—not about device firmware.

“Then are we allowed to use it?” Tara asked.

“Allowed is not the only question,” Amina said. “We should not borrow the weight of another struggle carelessly.”

They retained the quotation in a section about civic demand, attributed it accurately and refused to equate their litigation with the historical struggle that produced it.

Benjamin Franklin's often-quoted sentence about liberty and safety caused a longer dispute. The exact 1755 language was: “Those who would give up essential Liberty, to purchase a little temporary Safety, deserve neither Liberty nor Safety.” Celia wanted it on the submission cover. Siti objected that connected prediction gave her both liberty and safety; the quotation was routinely used to make trade-offs sound simple.

They moved it into a footnote explaining the dispute. The words remained famous. Their fame did not decide whether local storage, cloud security or a particular consent model was safer.

Mira came to value the prohibited-inference column most. It disciplined the human desire to make every earlier warning predict the exact present. Foreseeability was not prophecy. Historical resemblance invited investigation, not automatic judgment.

The final table occupied two pages. Behind it sat thousands of pages of device-specific evidence. That proportion felt right.

### The Offer to Tara

After the bus incident, a media company offered Tara a scholarship in exchange for exclusive rights to her story. The contract included documentary, dramatic adaptation, synthetic likeness and future-media rights. Jaya saw the tuition amount and stopped breathing for a moment.

The company representative called it an opportunity to inspire millions. He said Tara's courage could become a global symbol of human resilience. He also said the offer expired in forty-eight hours.

Leena could not represent Tara because her firm might later use the bus incident in Mira's case. She found an independent children's-rights lawyer. The lawyer explained that the scholarship was conditional, the likeness grant perpetual and the creative-control promise non-binding.

Tara wanted the scholarship. Jaya wanted to refuse. They argued in Celeste's closed canteen while the adults tried not to turn either choice into a lesson.

“It is my story,” Tara said.

“You are twelve.”

“I was twelve on the bus.”

“That is why they want it.”

The independent lawyer proposed a limited licence: verified factual interview, no synthetic child likeness, no invented dialogue presented as fact, withdrawal right before publication, fixed education fund not contingent on publicity and a five-year term. The media company rejected it, then returned after another broadcaster reported its original conditions.

Tara accepted the revised agreement. The resulting short documentary showed the rescue and the fear afterward. It included the bathroom because Jaya said stories should stop skipping it.

The education fund was placed in trust. Tara used the first permitted withdrawal years later to study human-factors engineering.

Mira watched the documentary alone. She paused at the frame where Jaya's open prosthetic hand hovered above Tara's foot while three bodies pressed the brake together. No single system or person had saved the bus. The escape existed in the overlap.

She wrote a phrase in her notebook: **no body is an island of control**.

It was not a quotation. It was a traceable sentence of her own, one she would repeat when the case became a novel, a lecture or a disputed memory.

Disclosure produced eighty-six thousand documents.

Leena hired two temporary reviewers and could afford them for ten days. Mira developed a search dictionary from port maintenance practice: euphemisms for failure, cost, delay and responsibility. Pell's engineering group used *graceful degradation*. Finance used *service continuity*. Compliance used *purpose preservation*. All three might describe the same architectural fact while concealing it from an ordinary keyword search.

They built a map of document families. A change request led to a capacity table; the table led to a finance meeting; its attachments referred to a regulatory workstream stored under a different project number. The archive was not a pile but a city designed without street signs.

On the eighth night, Leena fell asleep at her desk. Mira found the old Northbridge memorandum open beside her. Its disputed sentence was highlighted, but so was the missing condition two lines below.

Mira covered Leena with a coat and continued searching.

At dawn, Leena woke and asked whether Mira had read her private notes.

“No.”

“You could have.”

“That is not permission.”

The case was teaching them both that technical access and lawful authority were not synonyms.

Vanguard delivered them in a searchable archive with inconsistent dates, duplicated titles, and internal abbreviations omitted from the index. Leena called it lawful obstruction. Mira called it Tuesday.

After work, she read change requests and validation reports. At 01:17 on the sixth night, she found a reference in a meeting record:

`Residual dependency profile addressed in Appendix K.`

The filed safety assessment ended at Appendix J.

Hale, the support representative, appeared in the archive as recipient of a compliance script entitled `UNCHANGED USER / UNCHANGED CLASSIFICATION`. When Leena interviewed him, he insisted he had merely reassured anxious users. Then she showed him eighteen calls in which users asked about offline walking and he repeated language that did not answer them.

“I did not write the script,” he said.

“Did you know it avoided the question?”

Hale looked at Mira's transcript. “By the fifth call.”

He agreed to authenticate the guidance and testify that staff were instructed not to open material-change incidents without Compliance approval. Leena promised accuracy, not protection.

Hale had a daughter studying biomedical engineering. He told Leena he had stayed because the job included her tuition benefit.

“That explains the fifth call,” Leena said. “What explains the eighteenth?”

Hale looked through the bakery window toward the afternoon traffic. “By then I had repeated it enough that admitting the problem would also admit the earlier calls.”

Mira understood him immediately. Concealment did not require fresh cowardice each day. The first omission created a debt; every later omission was interest.

“Will you say that in the hearing?” she asked.

“If they ask.”

“Say it before they ask.”

Hale bristled. “You think because you finally reported, you get to grade everyone else's timing?”

“No,” Mira said. “I think they will ask why you waited, and an answer that sounds prepared will fail.”

He studied her, then nodded. “Eighteen calls.”

Vanguard responded that Appendix K was an engineering draft outside the final certification record.

The next morning, Jonah Pell waited outside Leena's office. He looked too young to have written anything capable of frightening a corporation.

“I authored Appendix K,” he said.

Leena brought him inside but made no promise of confidentiality until she understood his position.

Pell explained that Continuity Eight had begun as optional cloud refinement. Late in development, Vanguard reduced the local model so older implants could run the update without new hardware. The network did not merely improve prediction; it supplied computations removed from the local device.

“Why?” Mira asked.

“To support twelve thousand users on existing controllers. Replacement would have delayed deployment and tripled cost.”

“Your appendix called it material?”

Pell looked at the floor. “My first draft did.”

“And the final?”

“I changed it to performance-sensitive dependency.”

“Why?”

“My manager said material had a regulatory meaning I wasn't qualified to assign.”

“So you removed the word that required action.”

“I documented the architecture.”

“In an appendix they removed.”

Pell's face reddened. “I am here now.”

“Because?” Leena asked.

“Compliance intends to say implementation departed from approved design. They are preparing to blame the engineering group.”

Mira stood. “So this is not conscience. It is positioning.”

“Does that make the architecture false?”

She had no answer that helped her.

Pell's signed hash proved only that a document matching his description had existed at a certain time. It did not prove the document had been adopted, circulated to decision-makers, or correctly interpreted. Leena repeated this until Mira stopped calling the hash proof of concealment.

They needed the corporate review trail.

For four nights Mira searched metadata rather than prose. The archive used project codes inherited from three merged divisions. A meeting listed as `C8 Cost Harmonisation` contained no obvious safety reference. Its attendance record included Vale, Pell's manager, the clinical director and two finance officers. An action item directed Compliance to “resolve classification externalities before universal deployment.”

“Externalities,” Mira said. “That means us.”

“It may mean licensing cost,” Leena said. “Do not improve their evidence for them.”

The next document was a controller-capacity table. Replacing the local model would cost more and delay rollout by fourteen months. A finance note compared three options: hardware replacement; limited rollout to new controllers; or network migration under existing certification. The third was selected.

Beside the selection, someone had written: `Regulatory posture manageable with purpose continuity.`

The author field was blank, but the revision history was not. Vale had opened the file twice and approved the meeting record that incorporated it.

It still did not prove he wrote the phrase. It proved he knew classification cost was part of the deployment decision.

Pell possessed a signed hash of the appendix and a message from Vale directing that dependency language be relocated from the executive risk summary to technical supporting material. He had no lawful copy of the full document. Leena would need to compel Vanguard to produce it and authenticate it through corporate records.

“If he testifies,” Mira said after Pell left, “they will tear him apart.”

“Probably.”

“He changed the report.”

“Yes.”

“He waited until they threatened him.”

“Yes.”

“You still want him.”

Leena gathered the documents. “Courts would be empty if evidence were accepted only from the morally complete.”

The CSRA hearing officer ordered Vanguard to produce Appendix K for the classification proceeding.

Its final line read:

`Existing fallback satisfies emergency stability criteria but may not sustain ordinary unaided mobility during prolonged network absence.`

Vale had approved the assessment twelve days before Continuity Eight was deployed.

Vanguard moved to exclude the appendix as a draft and to restrain Pell from discussing protected architecture. The hearing officer refused the broad restraint but placed detailed source material within a confidentiality ring. The public could know the nature of the dependency; competitors would not receive code.

The order mattered to Mira. Lex-Cybernetica required reviewable evidence, not the destruction of legitimate confidentiality.

The night before the classification hearing, Leena received Vanguard's expert report. Dr. Morrow had discovered that Mira's unauthorized diagnostic setting increased log-writing activity during handover events. On her controller generation, that activity could consume enough local processing capacity to add between forty and one hundred and twenty milliseconds of motor delay.

“They can say I caused it,” Mira said.

“They can say you aggravated one category of delay after enabling diagnostics.”

“That is what the headline will say.”

“We are not trying the headline.”

The first Berth Nine failure occurred before Mira changed the setting. The service-link delay occurred after. Population reports from users without diagnostic access remained relevant. But the new evidence damaged the simplicity of her personal narrative and could reduce compensation further.

Mira wanted Leena to argue that the effect was negligible.

“Is it?” Leena asked.

“Compared with eleven seconds?”

“That is not an engineering answer.”

They called Sen at midnight. She confirmed Morrow's benchmark was plausible and proposed an independent replication. Mira agreed.

The result arrived at dawn: the diagnostic mode added a median of eighty-six milliseconds during specific handovers. It did not explain multi-second queues, loss of local-mode controls or aged-cache degradation. It did establish that Mira had made a compromised system slightly worse.

At seven, Mei Lin appeared at Leena's office carrying breakfast.

“I read the new report,” she said.

Mira waited for judgment.

“You made a mistake after their mistake.” Mei Lin handed her a container. “Eat before you explain the difference.”

The estrangement ended without absolution.

## Chapter Nine — Clear and Convincing

### The Appendix Was Not Missing

The document everyone called the missing appendix had never been deleted. It had been separated.

Pell found the first fragment in a procurement workspace labelled **Commercial Sensitivity — Resilience Options**. Finance held a cost table without the engineering narrative. Engineering held a narrative whose cost column linked to an expired workspace. Compliance received a slide saying local fallback was “under assessment.” Senior management received a decision page recommending “continuity-first architecture.” No single document lied. No ordinary reader could reconstruct the decision without access to all four.

Leena called it distributed omission.

“Is that a legal doctrine?” Mira asked.

“Not yet. It is a description.”

They built a chronology. At 08:10 on 3 March, engineering recommended contextual local controllers for SII-2 and above. At 11:42, finance estimated deployment would delay Continuity Eight by five months. At 14:06, Vale asked whether the resilience option was “launch critical.” At 16:31, product counsel replied that existing contracts permitted service modification, subject to clinical safety. The next morning, clinical safety asked for a formal dependency reclassification. That request entered a queue marked post-launch validation.

“They did not reject it,” Mira said.

“They launched before answering it,” Leena replied.

The distinction mattered. A rejected warning has an identifiable decision-maker. A deferred warning can circulate indefinitely while the product acquires momentum. Everyone may claim the issue remained open; users experience the architecture as closed.

The review team worked in shifts. Temporary reviewers flagged documents; Mira translated port terminology; Amina searched for identity substitutions; Lim examined failure-state diagrams; Celeste identified descriptions that treated lost work as minor inconvenience. Jaya could participate only after night shifts and often fell asleep at the table. They paid her from the litigation fund for every review hour. She initially refused, saying others volunteered.

“Your unpaid work would make our case about exploitation depend on exploitation,” Leena said.

Jaya accepted.

At 2:40 one morning, Tara woke in the canteen booth and asked why her mother had to work twice to prove she worked once. No one found an answer suitable for a child. Amina wrote the question in the review log.

They discovered a vocabulary of minimisation. *Non-persistent* meant the failure ended when service returned, regardless of what happened during it. *No clinical injury* excluded falls caught by harnesses and speech lost during irreversible moments. *User-retained mobility* counted a person as mobile if another person could carry them. *Alternative communication available* included screens a patient could not see.

Mira began replacing each phrase with a factual question. How long? What function? What consequence? Available to whom? Under what conditions? The questions prevented outrage from becoming vagueness.

Pell's name appeared on the resilience recommendation and again on the decision page. On the first, he wrote that eliminating certified local fallback would change functional dependence and might raise SII. On the second, his approval box was ticked.

Leena called him.

“I did not tick it,” Pell said.

Metadata showed the box had been completed by his delegated project account while he was in surgery. Company policy permitted delegates to approve scheduling items but not clinical architecture. The interface displayed no distinction between the authorities.

“Can you prove you were in surgery?” Mira asked.

Pell stared at her.

She realised she had asked for his medical records with the same instinct that Vanguard applied to everyone else.

“No,” she corrected. “Can the approval be tested without disclosing why you were absent?”

Leena requested access logs, delegation policy and calendar records limited to availability. They proved Pell had not authenticated during the relevant period. The reason remained private.

“You almost used me,” Pell said later.

“Yes.”

“You stopped.”

“After asking.”

“That is why we need procedure and not virtuous people.”

The comment entered Mira's notebook.

At dawn on the eighth review day, Amina found a phrase in a customer-impact worksheet: **voice-personality continuity—reputational only**. A column beside it assigned zero safety weight. The author was not hostile. An attached note recommended future user research and described personal voice as important to acceptance. But the scoring system could record only physical injury, task completion and system availability. Importance without a numerical field vanished from the decision.

Amina closed her laptop and walked outside. Mira followed at a distance.

The street behind the bakery smelled of flour and rain. Delivery vans arrived. Amina stood beneath an awning, her controller disconnected, making no sound.

Mira waited.

After several minutes, Amina typed on her phone: `They did not hate my voice. They had nowhere to put it.`

Mira replied: `Does that make it better?`

`It makes the remedy harder.`

A hateful author could be removed. A measurement system required redesign. Amina returned upstairs and drafted an evidence category for identity-bearing function.

That afternoon, an anonymous message arrived with a link to Vanguard's internal archive. It promised the complete appendix and executive correspondence. Leena told everyone not to open it. The link might contain unlawfully obtained data, malware or privileged material. Mira copied the message header without following the URL and notified the court.

Social media accounts published excerpts an hour later. One purported email showed Vale writing, *Users do not need to understand substrate architecture; they need continuity.* Another showed Pell calling fallback advocates “analog romantics.” Reporters demanded comment.

Pell denied the phrase. Vale said the material was fabricated. Supporters urged Mira to share it before Vanguard secured an injunction.

She refused.

For six hours, refusal looked like cowardice. The leaked sentences spread while the claimant group said only that it relied on authenticated evidence. Donations slowed. Commentators accused Leena of protecting corporate secrecy. Celia argued they were losing the narrative.

At 9:20 p.m., a forensic examiner reported that the Vale email combined text from two genuine messages written months apart. Pell's phrase was invented. Metadata had been stripped. Some attachments contained real patient identifiers.

The leak was a trap whether planted deliberately or assembled by an angry outsider. Had Mira published it, Vanguard could have challenged the group's judgment and patients would have lost privacy.

“We escaped,” Celia said.

“We waited,” Leena replied. “That is not the same as being lucky.”

The next day they disclosed the attempted leak, the forensic finding and their own internal disagreement. Public support recovered slowly. Trust built by restraint was less dramatic than trust built by revelation and harder to destroy.

Three days later, lawful disclosure produced the genuine email. Vale had written: *Users do not need to understand every layer of substrate architecture to experience continuity; clinical teams do need to understand any new dependencies before consent.* The second clause had disappeared from the fabricated version. Yet evidence showed his team launched before clinical teams received that understanding.

The truth was less villainous and more damaging. Vale had stated the correct standard and failed to meet it.

### Pell's Choice

Pell could remain a company witness, disclose within court orders and retain legal support. Or he could resign, become an independent witness and risk ruinous claims for confidential information. Activists described the choice as courage against money. Pell knew it was also healthcare against uncertainty: his partner required treatment covered by Vanguard's plan.

Mira found him on the pedestrian bridge above Tanjong Pagar Road. He watched buses move through the junction.

“Leena says I should not advise you,” she said.

“Good.”

“I came to make that clear.”

“Very useful.”

Pell told her about his partner, Sam, and the treatment schedule. Mira understood why his approval absence had been private. She apologised again for asking.

“If I stay,” Pell said, “they will call me captured. If I leave, they will call me brave and Sam will call the clinic about payment.”

“Can you testify truthfully while employed?”

“I think so.”

“Then perhaps employment is not the moral fact people want it to be.”

Pell remained. He negotiated a written non-retaliation agreement, separate counsel and continued health coverage regardless of testimony. Vanguard agreed because the tribunal was likely to order similar terms.

On the stand, he refused both sides' preferred story. Engineering warned about changed dependence. The risks were uncertain. Continuity Eight produced genuine benefits. Local controllers created security and maintenance costs. Vale understood the reclassification question and believed existing clinical processes could address it after launch. Pell believed that decision was wrong but not irrational.

“Why did you not stop deployment?” Leena asked.

“I did not have unilateral authority.”

“Did you threaten resignation?”

“No.”

“Why?”

Pell looked toward Sam, seated at the back. “Because I was afraid of losing things that had nothing to do with engineering.”

“Did that affect your judgment?”

“It affected my action.”

Tan established that Pell continued working on Continuity Eight and received a performance bonus after launch.

“You accepted the benefit,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You object now because the programme became controversial.”

“I objected in the appendix.”

“You stayed.”

“Yes.”

“You were part of the decision.”

Pell paused. “I was part of the system that made it possible.”

That answer denied him innocence and gave the tribunal the organisational truth it needed.

After testimony, Vanguard removed Pell from product decisions pending review but kept his salary and coverage under the agreement. Activists called it disguised suspension. Pell used the time to write a technical standard for auditable fallback.

Sam's treatment continued. No cinematic sacrifice purified the case. Pell remained compromised, useful and accountable.

### The Forty-Seventh Folder

The decisive document was not an email. It was a folder index generated automatically when finance migrated storage.

Folder forty-seven listed a deleted presentation titled **SII Exposure and Consent Strategy**. The deletion was lawful routine retention unless a litigation hold applied. Its date fell two hours after Vanguard received Mira's preservation notice.

Leena sought forensic recovery. Vanguard said the presentation was duplicative and deletion automated. Justice Venn ordered a narrow examination to avoid exposing unrelated trade secrets.

The recovery took four days. During that time, both sides prepared arguments about a file nobody had read. Mira imagined a confession and repeatedly reminded herself that anticipation was not evidence.

The restored presentation contained no order to hide failures. It contained something more institutional: a decision tree advising teams to avoid SII reclassification where performance remained within clinical range, because reclassification could trigger consent, redundancy and reporting obligations. One slide described higher classification as “regulatory expansion without user-perceived benefit.”

At the bottom, in small type, an engineer had added: **User perception may be constrained by non-disclosure of architectural dependence.**

The note was moved to speaker comments in the final version and not presented.

Vale had attended the meeting. His handwritten tablet annotation read: **Risk is not worth launch delay; handle through post-market monitoring.**

Those seven words became the centre of the hearing. Vanguard argued that *risk* referred to uncertain classification risk, not bodily danger. Leena agreed the grammar permitted that reading. She did not need melodrama. The annotation established that Vale knew reclassification was unresolved and chose deployment before resolution.

Mira read the words until they stopped looking like language. Vale had once sat on hot concrete to fix a reflected-sky error. Now scale had taught him to convert individual uncertainty into post-market data.

“Do not make him a monster,” Amina said.

“I am trying not to.”

“Try harder. Monsters let institutions call this exceptional.”

The genuine document went into evidence. The tribunal declined sanctions for deliberate destruction because automation and hold timing remained ambiguous, but it criticised Vanguard's retention controls and drew no adverse inference beyond the recovered content.

Leena considered that fair. Mira wanted punishment for the four days of fear and the months of concealment. Law separated her desire from what could be proved.

On the final review night, the group sat in Celeste's canteen beneath lights that hummed on local power. Tara slept across two chairs. Lim repaired the fan again. Amina checked the transcript. Jaya counted wages she had lost by attending. Pell sent a correction to one diagram. Siti submitted an objection to the proposed remedy.

Mira looked around the room and understood that the case had not assembled a movement from identical victims. It had created a temporary institution from people with conflicting needs who agreed to preserve one another's facts.

That was smaller than unity and stronger than a slogan.

The CSRA hearing room near Jurong Gateway resembled a laboratory that had learned courtroom manners. Mira had expected grandeur. Instead there were cable channels, modular desks and a wall clock two seconds fast. Nine affected users sat behind Imani. Siti sat behind Vanguard as its benefit witness.

Siti testified that Continuity Eight reduced her spasms. Leena established that Siti had chosen continuous prediction after individual clinical advice; universal-rollout users received neither the advice nor the choice.

### The People Behind the Variance

Justice Venn had ruled that the four incident-group members could testify only to their own experiences. They could not diagnose one another, speak for all eHumans or offer technical causation. Leena welcomed the boundary. She had watched too many public cases convert individual people into a chorus whose apparent unity made every difference look like contradiction.

Jaya testified first. She wore her cleaning uniform because she had come directly from work and because Vanguard's pleadings repeatedly called her an “occasional service user,” as though the hand became less integrated when she clocked out.

Leena placed Tara's steel lunch box on the evidence table. Jaya identified it as the object used during the supervised test. She described the grip release, the clinic cradle and the note inside. She did not describe herself as brave. She explained that she cleaned washrooms, glass partitions and executive kitchens; that each task required different force; and that an unexpected release over a sink was inconvenient while the same release on a ladder could be fatal.

Vanguard's counsel, Elise Tan, approached carefully. “Your prosthesis has occasionally released objects since it was fitted twelve years ago?”

“Yes.”

“So release events pre-date Continuity Eight.”

“Different events.”

“That is your impression.”

“That is my hand.”

Tan showed three maintenance records. One involved a worn thumb actuator; another a depleted battery; the third an object beyond rated weight. Jaya agreed with each.

“And you did not sue then?”

“The technician replaced the actuator. I charged the battery. I stopped lifting that bucket. A reason was given and a remedy existed.”

“Vanguard also offers remedies.”

“Vanguard told me to grip less firmly when its system was opening my fingers.”

Tan displayed a help-desk transcript in which Jaya had written *maybe I held too tight*. “Those were your words?”

“Before the test.”

“You changed your account after meeting Ms Chen.”

“I changed my account after evidence.”

Mira saw several reporters look up. Jaya noticed none of them.

“You have become active in a campaign against connected devices,” Tan said.

“No. My daughter prefers connected calibration because it makes braiding easier. I prefer a hand that tells me before it changes who controls the fingers.”

Leena's re-examination took one question. “What do you want the tribunal to prohibit?”

“Secret dependence.”

Jaya left the witness table and went back to work before closing submissions.

Amina testified through both her personal voice model and a neutral text display. At her request, the transcript identified which channel delivered each answer. Vanguard objected that the distinction could prejudice the tribunal by dramatising a service feature. Justice Venn replied that the medium was part of the alleged harm.

Leena asked Amina to describe her father's final night. Before answering, Amina made three limits explicit.

“The outage did not cause his stroke. It did not cause his death. I seek no finding that more speech would have extended his life.”

“Then why is the event relevant?”

“Because Vanguard measures its failure as fourteen minutes of reduced service. The same fourteen minutes contained the last question my father ever asked me.”

She recounted the bedside, the unreadable screen, the generic authorisation message and the nurse reading words her father wanted to hear from his daughter. The hearing room remained silent except for the court typist.

Tan did not challenge the grief. She challenged the property claim. “Your contract licensed the personal voice model. It did not transfer ownership of the underlying synthesis software.”

“I do not claim your software,” Amina said.

“The voice cannot be generated without it.”

“My body cannot speak without it. Those are different sentences.”

“You accepted cloud verification as a security control.”

“I accepted a service description saying verification protected my model from impersonation. It did not say failure would prevent me from using the model locally.”

“A local copy could be stolen.”

“Then give me the choice between theft risk and speech risk. Do not make the choice, hide it, and call the result protection.”

Tan presented the recording made by the nurse. Amina's forced breath could be heard beneath the system's repeated message. Leena had warned that hearing it in public might be unbearable. Amina had replied that concealment was more unbearable.

Halfway through playback, Mira looked down. Amina typed a message to her across the claimant table: `Do not look away for me.` Mira raised her eyes.

After the recording, Justice Venn recessed for ten minutes. The official reason was an equipment check. When she returned, her voice was composed and her questions were exact.

“Would continuous access to the personal model eliminate all relevant risk?”

“No. A corrupted model could misstate instructions. A stolen model could impersonate me. Local storage could fail.”

“What remedy do you seek?”

“A disclosed choice, an intelligible fallback, and a rule that identity-bearing functions count when systems assess harm.”

She did not ask the tribunal to restore the goodbye. Law could not. She asked it to stop treating similar losses as zero.

Lim entered with a cane and declined the powered chair offered by the court. His stairwell descent had made him briefly famous. Vanguard played the broadcast clip in which he joked about the umbrella's safety margin.

“You appeared cheerful,” Tan said.

“I had reached the ground.”

“No person suffered physical injury.”

“Because people carried the consequence your system declined to carry.”

Tan showed that the utility fire was contained and firefighters reached Lim before he faced smoke or flame. “So with hindsight, remaining in your flat may have been safer.”

“Safety instructions told us to leave.”

“But objectively—”

“A resident cannot evacuate by hindsight.”

Lim explained machines as he had taught apprentices: every safety device embodies a decision about which failure is tolerable. A fuse sacrifices continuity to prevent heat. A brake sacrifices motion to prevent collision. A local controller may sacrifice optimisation to preserve agency. There was no neutral safe state; there were only choices, designers and people who carried the cost.

Vanguard's expert objected that Lim was giving engineering opinion. Justice Venn sustained the objection as to general causation but permitted Lim to explain his own understanding and conduct.

“I am not qualified in neural computation,” Lim said. “I am qualified in descending my own stairs.”

The final witness was Celeste. Of the four, she feared the hearing most. Her immigration history, debts and irregular income were irrelevant to the device claim, yet disclosure had gathered them into Vanguard's files. She believed one wrong answer could cost her job or housing.

Mira found her in the corridor holding a paper bag that had gone translucent with oil. “I brought curry puffs,” Celeste said. “Then I remembered nobody eats before cross-examination.”

“Lawyers do.”

“Lawyers are the reason nobody else can.”

Mira told her she could withdraw. Celeste asked whether the test report would remain evidence. When Mira said yes, she considered leaving. Then Tara arrived from school with Jaya. The girl held up two uneven braids.

“Ma did both today,” she said.

Celeste looked through the hearing-room glass at the rows of company lawyers. “I want to cook at my daughter's wedding. If I leave, they will say this is about a pot.”

On the stand, she described the lost breakfast shifts. Her base hourly rate had not changed; therefore Vanguard's model recorded no wage injury. But she lost the early-hours premium, customer tips and the informal authority that came from opening the kitchen. A younger worker gained the shift and was blamed by colleagues for replacing her.

“Did your employer discriminate against you?” Tan asked.

“My employer saw a risk.”

“A real risk?”

“Yes.”

“Then removing you from hot service was reasonable.”

“Until the system was fixed.”

“Your employer could not fix a medical device.”

“Exactly.”

Responsibility had been divided so finely that no one held enough of it to act. The employer blamed the device. The manufacturer blamed the continuity provider. Vanguard blamed networks and individual configuration. The insurer called lost premiums variable income. Each statement contained truth. Together they formed an escape route.

Leena asked Celeste what the system enabled before Continuity Eight.

“Work. Chopping onions. Writing labels. Holding my grandson when he was small.”

“And what would you choose now?”

“Connected correction while it works. Local correction near heat. A warning before either changes.”

“Do you want fewer enhancements because you earn less?”

Celeste looked at the tribunal. “I want the same safe choices. If public money cannot buy every improvement, say so openly and prioritise need. Do not quietly give poorer people a cheaper body and call it proportionate.”

That answer forced the hearing beyond Mira's remedy. Under the Lex-Cybernetica guideline, SII classification determined oversight duties, but it could not lawfully become a ranking of human worth or a mechanism for allocating weaker safety to people with less money. The greater the integration, the stronger the continuity obligation. Capacity to pay might affect optional enhancements; it could not reduce the minimum protection of bodily autonomy.

During lunch, Siti joined them. She had testified for Vanguard and expected hostility. Jaya offered her a curry puff.

“My update works,” Siti said.

“Good,” Celeste replied.

“If they suspend it, my spasms return.”

“Then they should not suspend it.”

Leena clarified that the revised application sought suspension only where adequate fallback, consent and risk review were absent. Clinically justified users who chose continuous prediction could retain it.

Siti read the requested order. “This changed.”

“The evidence changed us,” Mira said.

Siti looked at her. “Companies are not the only ones who hide revisions in appendices.”

Mira accepted the rebuke. The claimant group had discussed the narrower order but had not circulated it to benefit witnesses. They corrected that failure before the afternoon session by publishing a plain-language version and inviting objections.

Arjun said the local mode was too weak for jobs requiring rapid precision. Amina said voice identity needed a separate continuity standard. Lim said controllers stored in clinics were useless during midnight evacuations. For two hours, the supposed allies argued in a consultation room. The order improved.

Mira began to understand that coherence did not mean everyone wanting the same thing. It meant a rule capable of holding their differences without deleting any person to make the pattern neat.

“Would you accept SII-3 safeguards if they did not remove the service?”

“Yes.”

“And a tested local profile for Mira?”

“Yes.”

The supposed witness for Vanguard supplied the shape of a lawful remedy.

Technical displays occupied one wall. The independent assessor sat beside the hearing officer rather than with either party. The proceeding was regulatory, not a civil trial: the Authority bore the burden of supporting a contested classification by clear and convincing technical evidence, while each claimant still had to prove individual loss.

Vanguard's argument was simple enough to repeat in public.

Mira's biological condition had not changed. Her implanted hardware had not changed. The update improved a registered therapeutic system without altering its intended purpose. Therefore, its SII-2 classification remained appropriate.

Dr. Rafiq Morrow accepted that network dependence had increased. He disputed that it crossed the SII-3 threshold for the product as a whole. Short interruptions retained cached assistance, prolonged regional outages were rare, and higher classification might make beneficial features unaffordable.

The hearing officer asked whether rarity altered the severity of losing ordinary mobility.

“It alters expected risk,” Morrow said. “We should not classify solely by the most severe imaginable condition.”

Sen agreed with the principle and disputed the facts. Daily authentication, routine handovers and recorded regional faults made interruption foreseeable. A condition did not become unforeseeable because Vanguard's test ended before it appeared.

The hearing became a dispute over time. Vanguard measured outage in minutes. Mira lived in journeys, working days and the distance to an exit.

Morrow compared the complete SII structure. SII-1 and SII-2 generally described minimal or safely severable dependence. SII-3 required safeguards for recurring network integration. SII-4 addressed predominantly synthetic operation with a continuing organic component material to identity. SII-5 described fully post-biological systems, while eSapient recognition remained a separate legal determination.

“The tiers are not a staircase of beings,” he said. “They are drawers for engineering duties.”

Sen agreed. Their dispute concerned which drawer held Continuity Eight—not whether Mira had moved up or down as a person.

Leena began with the text of Section 3.

“Is the Enhanced Percentage a percentage of body mass?” she asked Vanguard's expert.

“No.”

“Does unchanged hardware establish unchanged functional dependence?”

“Not by itself.”

“Can software acquire greater authority over an Essential Function without adding a gram of material to the user?”

“In principle.”

“Did Continuity Eight require daily network authentication?”

“Yes, but—”

“Did the certified system require it?”

“No.”

The expert explained that local emergency stability remained available. Leena displayed the video of Mira crossing Sen's isolated walkway.

Vanguard objected that a single user's performance could not determine architecture for an entire product class.

The hearing officer agreed. The video was relevant to harm and disclosure, not sufficient by itself to establish classification.

Mira felt the room cool around her. She had wanted the four-minute walk to prove everything. The law required it to prove only what it could.

Morrow presented the replicated diagnostic result. Mira's restricted setting had added processing delay during certain handovers. Vanguard did not exaggerate it: her logs could not be treated as an untouched product sample.

Leena conceded the point, then separated the evidence. Berth Nine preceded diagnostic access. Nine other users had not entered the setting. Appendix K predicted aged-cache loss before Mira received the update. The diagnostic effect belonged in individual causation and damages; it did not erase architecture.

The hearing officer admitted Mira's logs with reduced weight for affected intervals. The ruling denied both sides the total victory they wanted from her mistake.

Dr. Sen testified next. She described the functions removed from local processing, the frequency of remote service calls, and the consequences of losing connection.

“Can you state the correct composite score?” Leena asked.

“Not to a scientifically responsible degree without access to the regulator's complete weighting method and Vanguard's population data.”

Vanguard's counsel almost smiled.

“Can you state whether SII-3 safeguards are indicated?”

“Yes. Continuous motor mediation, recurring network dependence, and material software authority are established. Where indicators conflict, the proposed framework applies higher-tier technical safeguards without changing civil status.”

On cross-examination, Sen agreed that Mira could remain conscious and perform limited movement offline.

“So the system does not control her,” counsel said.

“Control is not binary. Nor is dependence.”

Appendix K completed the architecture evidence. Support scripts proved Vanguard had trained representatives to answer classification questions by referring to unchanged hardware. The company had reasoned from a fact the index did not treat as decisive.

The CSRA ordered an event-driven reassessment of Continuity Eight and provisional SII-3 safeguards. Vanguard had to provide meaningful local operation, disclose offline limitations, and report related incidents.

It did not order universal local processing. For users such as Siti whose benefit depended on continuous prediction, Vanguard could comply through redundant providers, portable emergency computation and informed choice. The Right to Analog was a protected option and safety entitlement, not a command that every person must live offline.

But the hearing officer declined to award compensation. The administrative record did not yet resolve causation, employment loss, or Mira's contribution through delayed reporting.

Outside, reporters asked whether Mira had won.

“The system must be reassessed,” Leena said. “That is what we asked the Authority to do.”

Vanguard appealed to the Cyber-Jurisprudence Division before sunset.

It also sought a stay of the provisional safeguards, arguing that forcing an untested local controller into service might create the very danger the order was meant to prevent. Justice Venn refused to compel immediate deployment of unfinished software. Instead, she ordered a safer interim solution: no user could be disciplined for connectivity-related performance, critical workers had to receive portable local support, and Vanguard had to maintain redundant authentication until the appeal was decided.

The order turned a slogan into an engineering schedule. Sen's team would verify each interim measure. Worker representatives nominated by Imani would report failures confidentially. Vanguard retained room to design; it lost the power to define success alone.

Vanguard's appeal relied partly on Leena's old insurance memorandum. She considered withdrawing so another advocate could challenge it without personal distraction.

“Would that help the case?” Mira asked.

“It might help appearances.”

“Then stay and explain what you got right, what changed and what you should have foreseen.”

Leena filed an affidavit. Justice Venn excluded Leena's regret as irrelevant but admitted the memorandum's omitted technical condition. Leena lost the opportunity for public confession and gained the narrower legal point.

Mira looked at Leena. “Now have we won?”

“Now we have a judgment to defend and damages to prove.”

Mira had worked in logistics long enough to recognize a shipment halfway to its destination.

The company displayed the unauthorized diagnostic warning, her acceptance of it, the unreported failure at Berth Nine, and the incident report she had helped code as having no safety consequence.

Under oath, Mira could see how each decision had narrowed the next.

Before testimony, Leena gave three instructions: hear the whole question, ask rather than guess, and do not fight a premise before answering its fact.

“What if they make me look reckless?”

“You were reckless.”

Mei Lin waited outside with Arjun. She knew more about the service-link event from him than Mira had volunteered.

“You should have told me,” she said.

The train arrived.

That night the redundant authentication service failed during a planned migration. The interim portable controller kept Mira upright, but three users without completed distribution entered degraded mode. One was Arjun's training instructor on a remote crane console. He could not safely leave the chair, while a vessel departure window narrowed and supervisors pressed him to reconnect through an uncertified channel.

By morning, the outage log showed that Vanguard's distribution plan had prioritised executive demonstration sites over workers on overnight shifts. Leena filed it as evidence relevant to proportionality and remedy, not as proof of the original defect. Mira understood the distinction now: a true fact still had to be used for the right proposition.

Imani stopped her from leaking it. “You are angry they used people's data without proper purpose. Do not use the same people as evidence without asking.”

Leena sought an emergency variation. Justice Venn authorised a redacted allocation analysis to the CSRA and worker panel. Sen verified the pattern, and Vanguard was ordered to distribute controllers by functional risk rather than organisational rank.

The lawful route took nine hours longer than a leak. It produced an enforceable correction without publishing anyone's medical status. Mira found the delay almost unbearable. That was the point at which law ceased to be a speech and became discipline.

## Chapter Eleven — Risk Is Not Worth

### Forty-One Minutes

The night before Vale testified, the port's eastern control zone lost access to Vanguard's prediction service. A maintenance certificate had expired six hours early because one server interpreted midnight in Coordinated Universal Time and another used Singapore time. The fault was trivial enough to be embarrassing and broad enough to be dangerous.

Mira was at home under the interim local-mode order. Arjun called at 1:07 a.m.

“We have manual control, but Berths Seven through Eleven are stacking,” he said. “Two medical shipments are inside the queue. One is temperature critical.”

“Call the duty controller.”

“I am the duty controller.”

The promotion should have made her happy. Instead she heard the fear he was trying to compress into procedure.

“How many operators?”

“Nine. Three eHumans in service-degraded mode. No injuries. No collisions. Vale's office wants us to wait for token restoration.”

Mira opened the public vessel map. A monsoon cell was moving west from the Singapore Strait. At current speed it would reach the exposed yard before the refrigerated containers cleared.

“You have the paper degraded-mode book?”

“Revision six.”

“Revision seven changed the crossing priority.”

“Seven is on the network.”

There it was again: the fallback instruction depended on the system whose failure required fallback.

Mira could not issue commands. Her restricted-duty order and pending litigation made that clear. If she directed operations and someone was hurt, Vanguard could claim sabotage or unauthorised intervention. If she remained silent, the medicine might warm beyond certified limits.

“I can tell you what revision seven says,” she said. “You decide.”

“That sounds like a lawyer speaking through you.”

“It is.”

“I need the old Mira.”

“The old Mira hid a warning and put you on a defective link.”

The line went quiet.

Arjun asked her to describe, not direct. Mira explained that revision seven preserved a manual corridor between the cold store and Berth Eight, required two visual spotters at each crossing and prohibited simultaneous crane movement above the lane. Arjun repeated every condition. A second controller independently verified it from a printed training handout.

At 1:19, the first refrigerated carrier entered the corridor.

Celeste was working late in the canteen because an overnight vessel had brought extra crews. Her local tremor mode had not yet been issued. When the service failed, both hands began oscillating. She turned off the cookers, but a pressure kettle remained locked under heat. Its electronic release required the same building token service that had expired.

She called the emergency number. The automated system asked her to authenticate. She laughed once, sharply, then pulled the manual fire lever. That summoned the port brigade and triggered an evacuation of the canteen.

There was no fire. A supervisor ordered her to reset the alarm and threatened disciplinary action for false activation.

“The kettle is at pressure and I cannot control my hands,” she said.

“Then step away.”

“There are fifty workers in the shelter beside it.”

The supervisor attempted the remote release. Authentication failed.

At 1:26, the port brigade arrived. Its captain treated uncertainty as a hazard rather than an accusation. He cleared the adjacent shelter, isolated power and opened the mechanical pressure path with a shielded tool. Steam vented into the extraction hood. No one was harmed.

Celeste received a formal warning for misuse of the fire system at 2:10 a.m. The captain attached his own report stating that activation had been reasonable under the circumstances. The warning was withdrawn twelve hours later, after the report became public in the hearing.

Meanwhile, the first medical container reached the cold store. The second stopped at Crossing Delta when its vehicle map disagreed with Arjun's hand signals. Under revision seven, disagreement required a complete stop and an independent route check. Under Vale's emergency message, controllers were told to use “operational judgment to preserve throughput.”

Arjun could have waved it through. The route looked clear. Rain had begun, blurring the painted lanes. Every minute reduced the container's thermal reserve.

“What would you do?” he asked Mira.

She remembered Berth Nine, the warning she had hidden, and every person who had trusted her competence because it looked like certainty.

“Stop it.”

“You said you would describe.”

“Revision seven says stop.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“Then do not ask me.”

Arjun swore and ordered the stop.

The independent spotter walked the crossing and found a maintenance drone stalled below the vehicle's sensor horizon. It was small enough that a collision would probably not breach the refrigerated unit. Probably. The drone was removed. The carrier crossed four minutes later.

At 1:43, a lightning strike disabled one visual beacon. Arjun suspended the corridor. The medical logistics officer said the second shipment had nineteen minutes of validated thermal margin. A senior executive called from home and ordered movement to resume using vehicle sensors alone.

Arjun asked for the order in writing.

The executive refused and accused him of hiding behind process.

“Yes,” Arjun said. “Process is what we built to stand between your bed and my crane.”

He kept the corridor closed.

At 1:48, Jaya arrived to begin her cleaning shift and found staff crowding the assembly point. Tara was with a neighbour, so Jaya volunteered as a visual spotter. Her prosthetic hand was unreliable, but her eyes were not. Arjun initially refused because she was not operations-certified.

“I inspect your floors after everybody leaves,” she said. “I know every lane marker you have worn away.”

The safety officer paired her with a certified controller. Jaya saw that portable lamps placed for the corridor cast a reflection in standing water that resembled the disabled beacon. She repositioned them. The false line disappeared.

At 1:53, the brigade captain released two battery beacons from emergency stores. The corridor reopened. Both shipments reached powered cold storage with six minutes of validated margin remaining.

Later, headlines called it a six-minute miracle. Mira rejected that description for the same reason Lim had. The medicine survived because Arjun stopped for the drone, Celeste pulled an alarm others called false, a captain believed a worker's uncertainty, Jaya noticed reflected light, and written procedures prevented urgency from becoming permission to guess.

At 1:58, Vanguard restored the certificate. Continuity Eight reconnected across the port. Systems announced normal service as though the preceding forty-one minutes had belonged to nobody.

Mira slept for less than an hour. At dawn, she met Arjun outside the hearing room.

“Congratulations on duty controller,” she said.

“You heard that part.”

“I heard all of it.”

“The old Mira would have moved the carrier.”

“Yes.”

“It might have worked.”

“Yes.”

He waited for her to say the safer choice proved moral growth. She did not. The pause had consumed almost all the thermal margin. In another case it could cost lives. Risk could not be removed, only allocated under imperfect knowledge.

“I hated you when you would not answer,” he said.

“I know.”

“Then I found the drone.”

Mira nodded.

“I still hated you.”

For the first time since Berth Nine, they laughed together.

### What Mira Owed

Leena used the outage to seek emergency preservation of the certificate logs. Vanguard agreed before the tribunal could order it. Vale's lawyers understood that resistance would look worse than disclosure.

The logs revealed no conspiracy. A systems administrator had entered the wrong validity range during a rushed migration. Automated review detected the mismatch but classified it as low priority because redundant servers were expected to overlap. They did not overlap because an earlier cost programme had consolidated certificate authorities.

“A villain would be easier,” Mei Lin said when Mira explained it.

“Vale approved the consolidation.”

“That makes him responsible. It does not make the administrator unreal.”

Mira obtained permission to visit the administrator, Dev Malik, who had been suspended. He was twenty-six, supporting his parents, and had worked sixteen hours before the error. Vanguard's public statement described “operator deviation from established controls.” The established control was a second-person review position left vacant during restructuring.

Dev expected Mira to blame him. She asked him to reconstruct the screen.

“I should have seen UTC,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I thought you would say anyone could miss it.”

“Anyone could. You did.”

“Vale says the architecture was sound.”

“It was built so one tired person's date field could stop a port.”

Dev began to cry with the quiet embarrassment of someone who had postponed it until a stranger arrived. Mira sat without touching him. Comfort was not absolution; truth was not cruelty. She told him about her hidden warning at Berth Nine.

“Were you fired?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“Did anyone get hurt?”

“No.”

“Then it is different.”

“The outcome is different. The decision still belongs in the record.”

Dev gave Leena the names of three reviewers who had warned about certificate consolidation. He did not possess the documents and refused to guess their wording. That restraint made him credible.

Vanguard later reinstated him after the CSRA found systemic staffing failure. He accepted, partly because leaving would deprive him of legal support and partly because he believed systems improved only when people who remembered failures remained inside. Mira disliked the decision. She respected it.

Her own finances were deteriorating. Restricted duty covered rent and treatment but not Mei Lin's home-care increase. The settlement remained open. Signing would solve immediate problems and silence claims broader than her own.

One evening, Mira found Mei Lin on the kitchen floor. She had reached for a fallen towel, lost balance and been unable to stand. Nothing was broken. She had waited forty minutes rather than press the emergency pendant because she knew Mira was meeting Leena about costs.

“Why didn't you call?” Mira asked, lifting her into a chair.

“Because every call has become evidence in your life.”

The sentence hurt more than accusation. Mira had turned their home into a litigation annex: labelled chargers, timestamped symptoms, sealed envelopes, no spontaneous visitors. Mei Lin had begun hiding ordinary weakness to protect the case from distraction.

Mira ran a bath, made tea and called the care service to increase visits, though she did not know how she would pay. Mei Lin watched her calculate.

“Take the settlement,” she said.

“It binds the group.”

“Then refuse it.”

“You just said—”

“I am telling you the price, not choosing for you.”

Mira sat on the bathroom floor where Mei Lin had fallen. The tiles were cold through her trousers.

“When I was injured,” Mira said, “I thought the worst part was needing help. It was not. The worst part was that people praised me whenever I made help invisible.”

Mei Lin reached down and rested a hand on her hair.

“Then stop performing independence for me.”

The next morning Mira told Leena to counteroffer: individual compensation without silence, payment for documented care needs, preservation of group claims, public correction of SII classification and an independently audited fallback programme. Vanguard rejected it within twenty minutes.

Mira sold her vehicle and paid three months of increased care. Arjun organised colleagues to cover transport without calling it charity. Jaya contributed nothing because she could not afford to and refused to apologise. Amina helped Mei Lin configure a text emergency system. Lim installed a mechanical pull cord beside the bath.

Dependence became visible in Mira's own house. It did not make the house less hers.

Adrian Vale chose to testify.

Outside court, Lattice users carried signs asking that Continuity Eight remain available. Siti stood among them. Across the pavement, Imani's panel demanded local safety and honest classification. Cameras framed them as opponents. Before entering, Siti crossed the road and handed Imani a bottle of water. They argued about redundancy costs, then returned to separate signs.

The case had not divided good users from misled ones. It had exposed different bodies needing different protections from the same provider.

He explained the arithmetic of access. Replacing twelve thousand local controllers would have cost more than the Lattice programme's annual clinical budget. Continuity Eight allowed older devices to benefit from improved models. Average falls decreased. Average fatigue tolerance improved. More workers returned to skilled employment.

“Was the programme beneficial?” Vanguard's counsel asked.

“Demonstrably.”

“Did you intend to harm Mira Chen?”

“Of course not.”

Leena did not challenge the averages.

“A beneficial programme must still comply with material-change rules?”

“Yes.”

“You approved Appendix K?”

“I approved an assessment containing it.”

“The appendix warned that ordinary mobility might not be sustained offline.”

“For some users under extended loss.”

“Why was that absent from the user notice?”

“We disclosed a safe degraded state.”

“Safe enough to do what?”

Vale paused.

The question had travelled from Mira to support, from support to Compliance, and from Compliance into court. It had still not acquired an answer.

“Safe from uncontrolled motor output and collapse,” he said.

“Could Mira reliably walk to an exit?”

“Individual performance varied.”

“Could she lift a cup?”

“That was not the emergency criterion.”

“Did the notice explain that?”

“No.”

Justice Venn asked the final question.

“Mr. Vale, if reclassification increased cost, did that permit Vanguard to retain a lower-risk designation after its own evidence showed higher-risk architecture?”

“We believed classification remained debatable.”

“Debate is the purpose of reassessment, is it not?”

Vale looked at Mira for the first time that day. “Yes.”

Leena accepted Vanguard's evidence that Continuity Eight reduced falls for many users. Benefit, she argued, made accurate classification more important. A system used by twelve thousand people could not rely on duties designed for a safely severable local device because recertification cost money.

Justice Venn asked whether one product could support tiered operational profiles. Both Sen and Morrow said yes, if architecture and user choice were independently verified.

That agreement became the practical solution. The network-dependent profile would receive SII-3 safeguards. A genuinely local profile could retain SII-2 treatment after restoration, testing and meaningful consent. The system would be classified by what it did, not by the marketing name shared across configurations.

The judgment was delivered two weeks later.

During the wait, Mira returned to inventory review. Arjun resumed training. Siti sent data from a redundant-service proposal. Mei Lin's renovation finished, and Mira installed a bathroom door that stuck.

“Humidity,” Mira said.

“Poor measurement,” her mother replied.

Mira adjusted the hinge until it closed without force. The task had a visible cause, a local tool and an immediate result. She had forgotten how restful repair could be.

On judgment morning she took the train. At every network handover her knee tightened. She recorded none of them. Evidence collection had been necessary; living permanently as her own instrument was not.

The court rejected Vanguard's appeal. Continuity Eight was a material architectural change because it transferred meaningful motor-prediction functions from the local device to recurring network service. Unchanged hardware and unchanged therapeutic purpose did not answer the functional test required by Section 3 and Regulation 1.3.

The court also found the phrase *safe degraded state* materially incomplete. Emergency stability was not equivalent to a tested Analog Fallback State capable of supporting foreseeable ordinary needs.

Mira's unauthorized diagnostic access did not cause the architectural defect. Her delayed reporting had, however, prolonged some employment loss. The court reduced that portion of damages without allowing Vanguard to escape responsibility for a risk it knew before she did.

Justice Venn's conclusion was brief:

> An SII tier is a direction to engineers, regulators, and custodians. It is not a verdict upon the person who depends upon the system. Increased integration may require increased protection; it never supplies decreased worth. The law classifies risk so that society does not classify lives.

The judgment continued: **Dependence disclosed is a condition a person may evaluate; dependence concealed becomes an instrument of control.** The cloud could assist a body without acquiring a claim upon it.

Vanguard was ordered to fund compliant local fallback, compensate Mira, restore her employment standing, notify affected users, and submit Continuity Eight to independent monitoring. A remediation fund would support migration and individual assessment.

The compensation order separated its reasons. Mira recovered the allowance lost after Vanguard already possessed Appendix K and restricted her rather than reassessing the system. Her recovery for the earliest period was reduced because she had concealed the first motor delay. Arjun received protected training credit for the week lost to the service-link investigation. The other users were given an expedited process, not automatic damages without proof.

The court referred the incomplete regulatory submission to the CSRA for possible administrative penalties and individual review. It made no finding of criminal guilt. Vale's knowledge supported corporate responsibility; punishment of any person required a separate process and proof of that person's own state of mind.

It rejected Mira's demand for punitive personal damages against Vale. His knowledge supported corporate and regulatory remedies, but the joined record did not establish every requirement for personal punishment. Mira felt that as a loss.

“A law that makes it easy to punish the person we dislike,” Leena said, “will make it easy to punish the person we become.”

Victory did not require the law to give Mira everything anger requested. It required Vale to answer within the evidentiary rules that also prevented her from being reduced to her mistakes.

Outside the court, someone asked Mira whether she was pleased to be officially declared more machine.

Mira stopped.

“No court declared that,” she said. “The court declared that Vanguard had more responsibility.”

It was the sentence the reporters carried.

## Chapter Twelve — Local Mode

Vanguard called the judgment a clarification.

### The Work After Winning

The judgment did not switch the world into local mode. It created deadlines.

Within fourteen days, Vanguard had to identify every system whose update changed functional dependence. Within thirty, it had to provide interim Analog Fallback States according to SII risk. Within ninety, an independent board would review consent, architecture and incident reporting. Compensation required separate proof. Employment disputes remained with their proper tribunals. Nothing in the order declared eHumans superior, diminished eSapiens or treated SII as a measure of human value.

Mira read the schedule before she read the declarations. A right without implementation was another attractive interface over an absent mechanism.

The first week exposed shortages. There were not enough certified local controllers. Clinics lacked calibration staff. Some devices had been designed so thoroughly around continuous prediction that a useful offline mode would require new hardware. Vanguard proposed priority by economic productivity. Imani rejected it. A hospital coalition proposed priority by clinical severity alone. Lim asked whether a mobile worker facing evacuation risk counted as clinical severity. Celeste asked whether a cook's proximity to heat counted. Amina asked whether the inability to speak at a hospital bedside would ever enter a physical-risk scale.

The independent board adopted a matrix rather than a queue. It assessed probability, immediacy, reversibility, third-party exposure, identity-bearing function and availability of non-digital assistance. Income and organisational rank were excluded. SII influenced the strength of review but did not decide it alone.

Mira disliked matrices that pretended judgment had disappeared. She insisted the board publish reasons and record dissent. Pell insisted engineers could reopen classifications when new evidence emerged without forcing users through litigation. Siti insisted benefit loss be counted as harm. Amina insisted personal voice be treated as function, not cosmetic output.

Their first meeting lasted nine hours and produced six pages.

“At this rate,” Vale's replacement said, “the ninety-day deadline is impossible.”

“Then report what cannot be completed and why,” Mira replied.

“The public will call that failure.”

“It is failure.”

“You want us to announce imperfection?”

“I want you to stop announcing continuity when you mean aspiration.”

The board missed one milestone by three days. It published the missed date, affected devices, interim safeguards and responsible officers. Newspapers reported the breach for a morning. No market collapsed. No patient riot followed. The world proved surprisingly capable of receiving an honest delay.

Jaya received the first contextual hold-safe prototype. The new controller assessed force, orientation, temperature and the consequence of release. It could not solve every context, so the user selected profiles: public transport, kitchen, ladder, child care, sleep. Every profile displayed its compromise in plain language.

During calibration, an engineer asked Jaya which profile mattered most.

“Braiding,” she said.

He assumed she was joking. Tara placed three ribbons on the table. Jaya explained that a braid required sustained low force, fine sensation and predictable release near a moving child's scalp. It was a demanding safety test disguised as affection.

The engineer added a braiding scenario to validation. Jaya completed the first braid slowly. The left side sat higher than the right. Tara examined herself in the mirror with solemnity.

“Again,” she said.

On the fourth attempt, the braids matched. Jaya laughed so suddenly that the engineer dropped his stylus. Tara turned and hugged her around the waist. The hand remained closed only as firmly as Jaya intended.

Vanguard wanted to use the moment in an advertisement. Jaya refused. She allowed the validation data to be published under an open safety licence and required that the scenario be called the Tara Test. Other prosthetic laboratories adopted it.

Amina's remedy proved harder. Security specialists warned that a portable personal voice model could be stolen, modified or used after death. Amina agreed. She helped design a two-part local vault: the user controlled one credential; an independent fiduciary held recovery authority; emergency phrases remained available without network approval; and every substitution announced itself before speaking.

The first prototype failed by adding a cheerful tone to emergency warnings. Amina rejected it. The second preserved identity but introduced a half-second delay that made conversation exhausting. She rejected that too. The third passed intelligibility, identity and latency tests but required expensive secure hardware.

Vanguard proposed it only for premium subscribers.

The board voted six to five against the proposal. Identity continuity was classified as a minimum protection where speech synthesis substituted for a lost bodily function. Optional celebrity voices could remain premium; the user's own functional voice could not.

On the day Amina activated the local vault, she returned alone to her father's ward. Another patient occupied his old bed. She stood by the rain trees and played the sentence she had been unable to say. Her own recorded cadence filled the quiet corridor.

“I am here. I love you. You taught me not to surrender my words.”

Nothing answered. The technology did not repair the past, and she would not let anyone claim it did. She saved the phrase as the vault's recovery test so every future version would have to preserve it exactly.

Months later, Amina won the course-leader position. Her first lecture began with the generic voice for one sentence, then her personal model for the next. She asked students what changed. They listed tone, identity, trust, history and authority.

“All of that,” she said, “was once classified as appearance.”

Lim received a certified home controller in a red case beside his front door. He organised monthly evacuation practices, infuriating neighbours until the building manager discovered that half the emergency lights had expired batteries. The boy who counted landings became the official floor marshal and demanded a whistle.

During the first drill, Lim stopped on the twelfth floor.

Celia went pale. “Is it locking?”

“No. I am tired.”

She laughed with relief, then became angry that relief could be so exhausting. Lim sat on the landing while residents passed.

“There is no prize for reaching the ground first,” he told her.

The controller displayed fatigue and suggested the evacuation chair stored two floors below. This time the chair was present, charged and reachable. Lim used it without treating assistance as defeat.

Celeste's return to hot service coincided with her daughter's civil wedding reception. She had promised to cook laksa for eighty guests. Her employer offered the canteen kitchen after hours, partly from generosity and partly because the tribunal had made generosity reputationally useful.

At four in the morning, the connected correction service failed during a regional maintenance window that had been disclosed in advance. Celeste's controller warned her, switched to the certified heat profile and reduced fine correction. She could choose to stop. Instead she assigned chopping to two relatives and handled seasoning while seated.

The broth was ready by noon.

At the reception, her daughter complained that it was too spicy. Celeste accused marriage of damaging her taste. Guests argued, children spilled drinks, and Lim asked for a second bowl. Nothing extraordinary happened. That was the happiness the remedy protected: an ordinary family quarrel around food made possible by a system that failed honestly.

Siti remained on continuous prediction. Her spasms decreased further after a later update. She carried a local controller but rarely used it because degraded mode was clinically painful. The consent form now stated that fact in the first paragraph, along with the risks of connection.

She joined the board as a benefit representative and disagreed with Mira often. Siti believed Mira's caution could harden into nostalgia for less capable systems. Mira believed Siti sometimes treated performance gains as consent. Their arguments improved both positions.

“You need me,” Siti said after one meeting.

“That is an alarming interpretation of governance.”

“You need someone who likes the thing you nearly banned.”

Mira could not deny it.

Arjun became permanent duty controller. He required every fallback instruction to exist on paper, on isolated local storage and in accessible audio. Staff mocked the paper binders until a software update corrupted the audio index. Then they mocked the colour of the binders instead.

His shoulder retained a small limitation. On wet mornings, pain travelled down his arm. Mira once offered to help lift a manual lever. He refused, then reconsidered and accepted.

“This does not mean I forgive Berth Nine,” he said.

“I know.”

“It also does not mean I never will.”

“I know.”

Their friendship returned as a practice rather than a verdict.

Dev Malik led a certificate-safety redesign. Every critical certificate now required independent review, automated timezone normalisation and an offline expiry map. He asked that the forty-one-minute outage remain in his personnel record together with the staffing finding. Vanguard wanted to expunge it as part of settlement.

“If you erase the error, you erase the reason for the safeguard,” he said.

The company preserved it in a protected learning record that could not be used for routine promotion decisions. Whether that compromise would endure under new management remained uncertain. The board scheduled an audit.

Pell testified before Parliament on the proposed adoption of Lex-Cybernetica principles. He did not present the treatise as enacted law. He called it what it was: a proposed and expected-law framework offering legislative guidelines for governance of eHumans, eSapiens and higher levels of cybernetic integration. He distinguished the tribunal's binding order from the framework that had helped articulate it.

One legislator asked whether SII labels would stigmatise people.

“They can,” Pell said. “Any classification can become a hierarchy if institutions forget what it classifies. SII describes substrate dependence and associated governance duties. It does not grade intelligence, morality, citizenship or human worth.”

Another asked whether SII should ever decrease. Pell used Jaya's controller as an example. A system might add robust local capacity and thereby reduce external dependence even while improving function. Reclassification had to follow actual architecture, not marketing claims or a one-way assumption that every enhancement increased dependence.

Mira watched from the gallery beside Imani. The hearing did not enact the entire framework. It sent selected provisions to committee for public consultation. Advocates called that timid. Industry groups called it rushed. Both descriptions ignored the real work: definitions, funding, appeal rights, technical standards, transition periods and enforcement.

“Are you disappointed?” Imani asked.

“Ask me after the implementation table.”

Imani smiled. “You have become impossible to inspire.”

“Inspiration is not version-controlled.”

They attended every committee session.

### The Cost Hearing

Compensation was determined six months after liability. Vanguard offered a common formula based on service downtime, documented medical expense and lost base wages. It excluded grief, lost premiums, household assistance and loss of identity-bearing function.

Leena challenged the exclusions but warned the group that damages could not become a price list for dignity. Money was an imperfect legal instrument. It could fund care and recognise wrong; it could not measure a goodbye.

Amina sought no amount for her father's death. She sought damages for loss of functional speech, the cost of local security and the distress caused by undisclosed substitution. The tribunal awarded a modest sum and a declaratory remedy stating that continuity of an identity-bearing assistive function was not merely cosmetic.

“Too little,” reporters said.

“Money was not the sentence I needed,” Amina replied.

Jaya recovered lost wages, device expenses and compensation for repeated loss of bodily control. She placed half in Tara's education account and used the rest to reduce night shifts. When a relative asked why she did not buy a better apartment, Jaya said time braiding hair was also property.

Lim recovered emergency-care costs and received a building-safety order rather than large personal damages. He called the order useful and the amount insulting. Both statements were true.

Celeste's lost shift premiums were difficult to prove because tips were not fully recorded. She refused to invent estimates. The tribunal used schedules, customer records and employer testimony to reach a conservative figure. Her employer restored seniority and created a device-failure leave category for all staff, including eSapiens using external medical equipment.

Mira's damages were reduced for contributory conduct at Berth Nine. Vanguard argued for a complete bar; Leena argued that Mira's concealment concerned one incident and did not authorise an undisclosed architectural change. Justice Venn allocated responsibility between them.

Mira felt anger when she read the reduction, then shame about the anger, then suspicion of the shame. Arjun ended the cycle.

“You wanted the record to keep your mistake,” he said. “It kept it.”

The remaining award paid Mei Lin's care, legal costs and a reserve for future controller replacement. Mira declined a confidentiality clause. The amount became public, along with the reduction.

Vale appealed the finding against him personally. The appellate court held that he had not designed the code and had relied on expert teams, but sustained responsibility for approving deployment after receiving the resilience appendix and for representations that omitted the known dependency. It narrowed one part of the reasoning: corporate officers were not strictly liable for every technical failure below them. Responsibility required authority, knowledge and a relevant act or omission.

Leena called the narrowing legally sound. Activists accused her of defending executives. She published a note explaining that law strong enough to govern enemies must remain accurate when anger prefers breadth.

Mira quoted Douglass at a public meeting—“Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will”—and then explained what the case had taught her about the sentence. A demand begins movement; it does not decide evidence, remedy or design. Power can be corporate, governmental, technical or personal. Claimants also exercise power when they speak for others. Every exercise requires limits.

An audience member asked why she qualified a famous saying instead of letting it inspire people.

“Because admiration is not a licence to stop thinking,” Mira said.

### A Different Berth

When Mira's restrictions ended, the port offered her a compliance position. It paid more and carried less physical risk. The role would make her responsible for auditing SII-linked workplace systems. Vale's successor expected her to accept.

She refused the first offer because it reported through corporate legal. The second reported through operations and still allowed conflicts to be buried as performance matters. The third created an independent safety office with direct access to the port authority and a protected worker panel.

Mira accepted that version.

Her first audit found that an exoskeleton used by cargo teams was labelled SII-1 because it was worn externally. In practice, predictive scheduling penalised workers who disconnected it, and their wages depended on its performance score. The physical interface was shallow; the economic dependence was deep.

The existing SII model did not capture the entire problem. Mira did not force it. She referred the economic coercion to labour regulators while recommending a separate Lex-Cybernetica review of functional dependence. A proposed law was useful when it illuminated facts, dangerous when treated as an answer to every question.

Her second audit found a higher classification than necessary because a vendor wanted the prestige of selling “SII-4 certified” equipment. Mira ordered the claim removed. Greater SII meant greater obligations, not greater personhood or better technology.

On the anniversary of the seven-minute delay, she returned to Berth Nine with Arjun. The latch had been replaced. The manual release now sat behind a transparent cover with instructions in four languages and tactile markings. A local control module occupied the cabinet beside it.

“Test?” Arjun asked.

Mira disconnected her network service under the approved protocol. Her leg shifted to local mode. A clear warning stated reduced precision, remaining battery and manual assistance options. Arjun isolated the lane. Mira walked to the latch.

It resisted at first. She changed stance, used both hands and pulled. The mechanism opened.

No one applauded. It was a scheduled test at an industrial berth, and scheduled tests are successful when they become uninteresting.

As they reset the cover, a junior operator asked Mira whether the case proved machines should never control bodies.

“No,” she said.

“That companies should?”

“No.”

“Then who controls them?”

Mira looked across the terminal: workers, cranes, networks, local modules, regulators, contracts, training cards and the physical sea moving beneath every digital map.

“Control is shared,” she said. “That is why responsibility cannot disappear.”

Vale resigned six weeks later. In his final statement, he defended the Lattice programme and accepted responsibility for “insufficient regulatory engagement.” Mira read the statement twice and decided that history could determine whether the phrase was an apology.

The CSRA later found Vanguard's omission serious and imposed a turnover-linked administrative penalty. Most of the proceeds entered the remediation and independent-monitoring fund. Vale was barred for three years from serving as compliance officer for a licensed high-risk integrator. The Authority declined to allege fraud because the evidence established deliberate minimisation, not every element of criminal deception. The distinction angered people who wanted a villain punished absolutely. Leena called it the discipline of law.

Vale requested a final meeting. He did not ask forgiveness. He showed Mira the programme's affordability projection: compliance would raise subscriptions unless Vanguard accepted lower margins or public procurement absorbed part of the expense.

“You still think I endangered the programme,” Mira said.

“I think the judgment forces an honest price,” Vale replied. “I should have forced it earlier.”

“Would you have deployed anyway?”

He considered the question. “Before this case, yes.”

It was not an apology. It was a precise account of the choice he had made and might have repeated.

Public procurement later conditioned support on open interfaces, continuity insurance and income protection during safety suspension. Vanguard preserved access by accepting obligations it had called unaffordable. Cost had not vanished. It had been moved away from the bodies least able to carry it.

The terminal offered her Berth Nine.

Mira observed a full shift before deciding. The work returned like remembered music: vessel windows, crane availability, yard density and decisions no model could fully own.

When the map and a carrier disagreed, Tomas isolated the lane before Mira spoke. Both records were preserved under the new protocol.

“You could come back,” he said.

“I know.”

She declined because competence did not require repeating the life she had fought to regain. The Integration Office was where reporting categories and suspension pay would be decided before the next person needed a lawsuit.

She declined.

Instead, she became the first worker representative in Vanguard's Independent Integration Office. The title was grander than the room, which contained two desks, a kettle, and a reporting system Mira insisted include the categories *network dependence*, *material update*, and *unknown common cause*.

Her first visitor was a cleaner whose augmented hand released its grip during nightly authentication. He apologized five times for wasting her time.

Mira showed him how to preserve the logs.

“Will I be suspended?” he asked.

“Possibly, while they assess safety.”

His face fell.

“And if that happens,” she continued, “your base income and position are protected under the remediation order. Reporting is no longer something you purchase with your wages.”

The order was not perfect. Workers still feared reassignment. Managers still preferred isolated explanations. Engineers still used language that made decisions sound like weather. But problems now had routes through the institution that did not require a person to become a public case.

Imani chaired the external user panel and never allowed Mira to describe it as reconciliation. Hale left customer support and became the panel's records officer after accepting an internal sanction for following the script after he knew it misled. Arjun completed his training and added mechanical isolation drills to the induction programme. Their futures were not prizes Mira distributed; they were consequences each had helped shape.

Siti remained on the network-dependent profile, now supported by two independently governed services and a portable safety module. She joined Imani's panel to oppose any rule treating disconnection as morally superior.

“Analog is a right,” she told its first meeting. “So is choosing connection with honest terms.”

Imani placed the sentence in the charter.

Tomas kept the supervisor position after an independent review. His promotion was delayed for a year, and his reporting authority was placed under audit. He and Mira ate together some Fridays. They did not speak of forgiveness as if it were a switch.

Jonah Pell joined a smaller medical-systems laboratory, where engineers were required to preserve dissenting technical conclusions beside management responses. Tomas remained under audit for a year, after which the worker panel confirmed his promotion. Mei Lin attended that meeting by public stream and later complained that Mira's office had no proper tea.

Dr. Sen sent Mira the final certification for Vanguard's new local controller with one sentence highlighted:

Before the test, Mira returned to the service link with Arjun.

“I came to say I was wrong to make you carry a risk I had not reported,” she said.

“You said that at the hearing.”

“That was evidence. This is an apology.”

Arjun rested both hands on the rail. “I wanted you to be either the person who saved me or the person who endangered me.”

“Which did you choose?”

“Both is inconvenient.”

He accepted her apology without pretending its consequence had vanished.

`Ordinary mobility and essential fine-motor functions sustained for not less than seventy-two hours without external network service.`

Mira received the controller on a grey morning in November.

The clinician offered to conduct the first local-mode trial indoors. Mira asked for the eastern pedestrian lane overlooking the terminal.

“There will be observers,” the clinician warned.

“Good.”

At 08:00, the network link was disabled.

No strength vanished. The assistance changed—slower prediction, firmer resistance at the knee—but it remained hers to direct. Mira took one step, then another.

Tomas walked several metres behind. The clinician stayed beside the rail. Leena watched from the gate, reading messages and pretending not to supervise.

Halfway across, Mira's left foot caught on an uneven joint. Tomas moved forward.

She lifted one hand.

He stopped.

Mira steadied herself against the rail. For a moment, her old instinct returned: refuse the rail, refuse the witnesses, refuse every visible sign that effort had a cost.

Then she adjusted her grip and continued.

Below, carriers followed their local routes. Above, the terminal map followed its predictions. Neither was assumed infallible. Where they disagreed, the system now stopped and asked a human being to examine the evidence.

Mira reached the end of the lane in two minutes and eleven seconds.

It was slower than the networked mode and faster than the sealed-room test. The number would enter a certification table. Engineers would compare it with thresholds. Regulators would decide whether it satisfied the standard.

To Mira, it meant she could reach an exit, make tea, cross her apartment, and choose when the network entered her movements.

“Again?” the clinician asked.

Mira looked back along the lane.

“Again,” she said.

This time, when Tomas walked beside her, she did not tell him to move away.

At the far end, Mei Lin waited with the chair from her apartment hallway.

“You will not need it,” Mira said.

“Then I will sit.”

Mei Lin did. Arjun checked the mechanical latch. Tomas logged the time. Sen reviewed the trace. Leena arrived late and accepted food from Imani without asking what was inside.

Mira understood that local control had never meant solitary control. The law had not returned her to a condition without dependence. No such condition had existed. It made dependence visible, contestable and chosen.

Below, an automated vehicle paused because its map and local route disagreed. The terminal did not blame the vehicle, conceal the discrepancy or continue on an average. It stopped, preserved both records and requested review.

Seven minutes later, the lane reopened.

Mira began the walk again.

### One Year and Seven Minutes

On the first anniversary of the judgment, the voluntary incident group met at Celeste's canteen without lawyers, regulators or press. The sign on the door said **PRIVATE FUNCTION**, though anyone looking through the glass could see Tara arranging paper cards and Lim criticising the chairs.

Mira arrived late by seven minutes.

Arjun checked the wall clock. “Should we preserve the event?”

“Already logged,” Amina said through her personal voice.

They had not gathered to celebrate victory. Amina objected to the word because her father remained dead. Lim said victory implied the stairs had disappeared. Jaya said any celebration requiring a correct legal vocabulary would end before dinner. Celeste served food and declared the question postponed.

Each person brought one object.

Jaya brought the steel lunch box from the laboratory test. The dent from its fall remained on one corner. She had declined a replacement because objects, unlike records, could show consequence without argument.

Amina brought a small offline voice vault. It contained emergency phrases, her teaching model and the recovery sentence for her father. A physical switch disabled every external interface. She passed it around but did not let anyone activate it.

Lim brought the repaired umbrella. The strap had been replaced by the manufacturer after the stairwell story reached its designers. They sent a letter explaining that it was not rated for human support. Lim framed the disclaimer and kept using the umbrella in rain.

Celeste brought the saucepan from Sen's test. It had never held hot liquid during the procedure, but the imagined arc from its rim changed how the tribunal measured risk. At home it now held a basil plant.

Siti brought nothing. “My evidence was that the system worked,” she said. “What object represents absence of failure?”

She placed her empty hands on the table. That was accepted.

Arjun brought the defective latch component from Berth Nine, released after investigation. Mira had not asked him to come. He set it beside her plate.

“This is not forgiveness,” he said.

“You have said that before.”

“You keep trying to make me efficient.”

Pell brought the original resilience diagram, printed from the version-controlled archive and stamped as declassified. Four paths led from network loss; three ended in unacceptable states. The fourth—certified local function—had once been coloured grey for deferred work. On the new standard, it was blue and mandatory where risk required.

Mei Lin brought no object because, she said, she had not survived eighty years to perform symbolism on schedule. She ate two curry puffs and told Tara to sit straight.

After dinner, they reviewed what had gone wrong since the judgment. Two local controllers had failed inspection because batteries degraded in storage. A clinic had copied consent wording without training staff to explain it. An employer had demanded SII status during recruitment and was under investigation. A manufacturer had reduced a device's nominal SII by renaming a remote dependency “supportive.” A wealthy user obtained premium redundancy within a day while a public patient waited three weeks.

“So the law failed,” Celia said.

“Parts of implementation failed,” Imani replied. “That is why enforcement exists.”

“That sounds like something officials say.”

“I am an official now.”

Imani had accepted appointment as the CSRA's first Director of Substrate Continuity. The group distrusted the title and trusted her enough to say so. She published her meeting calendar, recused herself from Mira's compensation matter and created a citizen panel selected partly by lot. Critics still accused her of importing Lex-Cybernetica into regulation without legislation. She responded by identifying which duties came from existing safety law, which came from the tribunal order and which remained proposals requiring Parliament.

The distinctions slowed speeches and prevented authority from being invented by enthusiasm.

“What worked?” Tara asked.

Emergency fallback time fell. Help-desk reports no longer closed automatically when service returned. Users could appeal SII classification. Identity-bearing functions entered harm assessments. Benefit-loss reports were reviewed alongside failure reports. No worker at the port had been removed solely because a controller entered local mode.

Most importantly, people began asking a new question before updates: *What will still work when this does not?*

The question migrated beyond eHumans. Hospitals applied it to records. Transit operators applied it to payment gates. Schools applied it to learning platforms. The rule did not demand rejection of networks. It demanded an honest account of dependence.

Mira worried that the phrase would become ceremonial. Vale's successor printed it on posters while cutting the training budget. She ordered the posters removed until the budget returned. The company complied after workers began photographing empty training rooms beside the slogan.

At nine, Tara asked everyone to write one sentence for a time capsule. Lim objected that a biscuit tin was not archival. Pell provided a sealed component case. Amina insisted on accessible digital and paper copies. Jaya said the project had become regulation and threatened to go home.

They wrote anyway.

Celeste: *A safe hand still needs fair work.*

Lim: *Fallback stored elsewhere is not fallback.*

Siti: *Do not protect me by taking the benefit I chose.*

Amina: *My voice is a function because my life uses it.*

Jaya: *Test the braid, not only the machine.*

Arjun: *A stop can be skilled work.*

Pell: *Every safe state chooses who carries the failure.*

Mei Lin: *Feed people before asking for principles.*

Mira stared at the blank card. She could have repeated the sentence reporters liked: greater dependence created greater responsibility. It remained true and had begun to sound complete when it was only a beginning.

She wrote: *No body is an island of control; therefore no responsibility may vanish into the connection.*

They sealed the case and stored it in the canteen's mechanical cabinet. The lock required no network, but Celeste wrote the combination on paper because mechanical certainty also failed when memory did.

On the way home, Mira and Mei Lin took the bus. At the interchange, the fare network went offline. Gates opened under the transit authority's public-access rule, and passengers continued without payment until service returned. An announcement explained the fault and the temporary procedure.

“Your revolution is free bus rides?” Mei Lin asked.

“Temporary continuity.”

“It needs a better name.”

They found seats near the door. Mira's controller shifted between networks and displayed a choice: retain local mode or connect to predictive service. The screen listed the consequence of each in plain language. She chose connection for the long walk from their stop, then stored a local map.

Mei Lin noticed. “Still frightened?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Courage without fear is usually marketing.”

At home, the bath pull cord Lim installed hung crooked. Mira promised to straighten it. Mei Lin forbade her, saying the crooked cord was easier to see. They argued until both were laughing.

Later that night, Mira received a message from Vale. He had not contacted her since losing the appeal.

`Daniel's clinic adopted the local-continuity standard. Too late for him. Not too late for others.`

Mira read the words several times. She did not know whether they were apology, accusation or information.

She replied: `The standard also preserves connected benefit when chosen. That part is not too late either.`

Vale answered after an hour: `Both are true.`

It was the nearest they came to reconciliation. Mira did not need more in order to continue, and she would not pretend less had happened.

The next morning brought a new report. An eSapiens warehouse planner using an external cognitive aid had been penalised after switching to offline mode during a privacy incident. The physical SII was low, but functional and economic dependence appeared high. Existing categories overlapped without resolving the labour issue.

Mira opened a file and wrote three headings: **what happened**, **which law applies**, **what the framework helps us see**. She did not begin with the answer.

Before lunch, Jaya called. Tara's school wanted to use the bus rescue in an assembly. Tara would participate only if Harish, the driver and the nurse were invited and if the school discussed fear after emergencies. Jaya asked whether that was too demanding.

“Power concedes nothing without a demand,” Mira said.

“You are quoting again.”

“Frederick Douglass. 1857. Different struggle, accurately attributed.”

“You have become unbearable.”

“Mei Lin agrees.”

At the assembly, Tara stood behind a lectern adjusted too high. She lowered it herself. The school played no dramatic music. Mr Tan described the moment before his memory disappeared. The nurse explained response without claiming certainty. Harish showed the repaired brace and the new emergency-use clause. Jaya described the hand opening. Tara described finding the pedal and being afraid afterward.

A student asked which person was the hero.

Tara looked along the row.

“That is the wrong shape of question,” she said. “If you choose one, you can forget to fix the rest.”

Mira, seated at the back, wrote the sentence down. She would ask permission before using it.

Outside the school, rain cleared suddenly. Sunlight reflected from buses, wet pavement and hundreds of windows. The brightness confused Mira's visual overlay for half a second. Her controller warned of reduced confidence and asked whether to pause prediction.

She paused it.

The world did not disappear. It became slower and required attention. Tara and Jaya waited at the crossing. Amina sent a voice message about the next board agenda. Lim complained in the group channel that the repaired umbrella had failed to open. Celeste posted a photograph of basil growing from the test saucepan. Siti challenged a fallback rule. Arjun reported that Berth Nine's manual latch had passed inspection.

Mira crossed when the ordinary green signal appeared.

The proposed law had not made her independent. It had made the relationships around dependence more honest. It had given institutions duties, users choices, opponents a forum and mistakes a record. It had also created new categories capable of misuse, costs requiring allocation and arguments that would outlive everyone in the first case.

That incompleteness no longer frightened her as it once had. A finished system was usually one that had stopped listening.

At the far kerb, Tara held out her hand. Jaya took it with the prosthesis. The contextual controller detected a child's fingers, limited force and preserved sensation. A small indicator confirmed local safety capacity while connected prediction remained available.

They walked toward the station.

No camera recorded them. No court observed compliance. No company counted the moment as benefit. Yet the hand held without asking a distant server whether affection was authorised.

For seven quiet minutes, nothing failed.

Then Jaya's hand issued a soft alert.

The contextual profile had detected rising actuator temperature. Earlier systems would have hidden the warning until performance crossed a threshold; the first Continuity Eight build might have requested remote guidance before explaining anything to Jaya. The new controller did neither. It displayed the evidence, reduced grip force gradually and offered three choices.

Jaya stopped beneath the station roof and showed Tara.

“We can wait,” Tara said.

“We will miss the train.”

“There is another.”

Jaya selected local rest mode. Her fingers opened by intention, not command. Tara moved to her other side and took her natural hand. The next train departed without them.

Mira watched the platform display count down eight minutes to the following service. Once, she would have treated the delay as failure. Now she saw the decisions inside it: a warning given before harm, a function reduced without being stolen, a child informed instead of reassured falsely, and time accepted rather than transferred invisibly to someone's body.

A maintenance worker approached and offered a seat. Jaya accepted. He asked whether the prosthesis needed emergency support. She explained that it was cooling normally. He logged the warm actuator only after she consented and showed her exactly which fields would be transmitted.

“The old form asked for the user's SII,” he said. “The new one asks for the device's functional state.”

“Better,” Mira said.

“More typing.”

“Also true.”

When the indicator turned green, Jaya flexed each finger. Tara offered one ribbon from her schoolbag. Jaya braided a narrow strand of the girl's hair on the platform while commuters moved around them. The braid was uneven. Tara inspected it in the dark glass of a vending machine.

“Again tomorrow,” she said.

The next train arrived. Its doors remained open long enough for Lim, moving slowly from the lift, to reach them. Amina was behind him, arguing through her personal voice with Celeste about whether basil belonged in laksa. Siti sent a message that she was already aboard and had saved seats. Harish appeared at the far end of the platform carrying parcels in his repaired brace.

They had not arranged to meet. The city had simply placed their routes together for a few stations.

Inside, no one discussed the judgment. Lim complained about the weather. Celeste distributed containers nobody had requested. Amina corrected an advertisement's claim that its service was “always available.” Tara did mathematics homework. Jaya's hand cooled against her lap.

Mira stood near the door because sitting made the Lattice stiff. Her controller remained local until the train cleared the crowded interchange, then asked again whether she wanted connection. She reviewed the conditions and chose yes.

Predictive assistance returned as a quiet alignment through her leg. It was useful. It was welcome. It was not surrender.

At the next stop, an elderly eSapiens passenger boarded with no implanted technology at all. Arjun, entering from another carriage, gave up his seat. The passenger thanked him and asked why so many people were carrying red controller cases.

“Long story,” Arjun said.

“Good one?”

He looked at Mira.

“Unfinished,” she replied.

The doors closed. The train moved south through the rain-washed city, carrying people whose dependencies were mechanical, digital, economic, familial and often unnamed. No index could contain all of them. No law could remove them. A careful law could decide who must disclose, who must prepare, who could choose, who could appeal and who answered when design became destiny without consent.

Mira felt the network predict the curve before the train entered it. Her local controller held the last safe state in reserve. Both capacities existed together.

That, at last, was the future she had been arguing for.

## Lex-Cybernetica Terminology Used in This Novel

This concise reader's guide does not replace the complete proposed framework at [Lex-Cybernetica](https://duck-14.gitbook.io/lex-cybernetica).

- **eHuman:** A non-statutory social term used here for a biological person with cybernetic integration. It does not create a separate class of personhood.
- **eSapient:** A recognized non-biological synthetic person demonstrating the autonomy and continuity required by the framework.
- **Cybernetic Integration (CI):** Hardware, software, synthetic tissue, prosthetic systems, neural interfaces or remote computation functionally integrated with a person.
- **Substrate Integration Index (SII):** A technical-risk classification based on functional mediation, remote dependence, software authority, reversibility and consequences of failure. It does not measure intelligence, dignity or legal worth.
- **SII-1 — Biological Baseline:** No integration or limited corrective or assistive integration with minimal functional dependence.
- **SII-2 — Functional Hybrid:** Significant augmentation that remains primarily local and safely severable.
- **SII-3 — Networked Integration:** Continuous mediation, material software authority or recurring network dependence with an independently operable safety state.
- **SII-4 — Substrate-Transition Array:** Predominantly synthetic operation with a continuing organic neural component material to identity or consciousness.
- **SII-5 — Fully Post-Biological System:** Continued consciousness requires no functioning biological substrate; eSapient recognition is separately determined.
- **Essential Function:** A capability necessary for life, health, movement, communication, memory continuity or meaningful civic access.
- **Material Control:** Practical power to initiate, prevent, redirect or terminate relevant conduct.
- **Cognitive Telemetry:** Data recording or inferring neural activity, intention, attention, emotion, memory or cognitive state.
- **Right to Analog:** The right of a biological person with CI to enter the safest technically feasible local state without losing Essential Functions.
- **Analog Fallback State (AFS):** A tested safe local state available after network loss, authentication failure, insolvency or payment dispute.
- **Substrate Parity:** Equal protection of autonomy and existence without making dignity depend on biological or synthetic composition.
- **Cyber-Substrate Regulatory Authority (CSRA):** The proposed independent regulator responsible for licensing, standards, incident review, continuity and enforcement.
- **Cyber-Jurisprudence Division (CJD):** The proposed specialist judicial forum for review and adjudication under the framework.

## Background Note

The novel's Tuas setting is grounded in publicly described plans for Singapore's port of the future, including electrified automated yard cranes, automated guided vehicles, remote management from the Tuas Port Control Centre and private 5G support. Present-day Singapore's judiciary comprises the Supreme Court, State Courts and Family Justice Courts; the Supreme Court includes the Court of Appeal and the High Court, whose General Division can hear civil matters and appeals from tribunals. The future Cyber-Jurisprudence Division in this novel is an imagined specialist list integrated into that recognisable structure.

Singapore's present Work Injury Compensation Act provides a no-fault statutory route for eligible work-related injury claims, including defined medical expenses and compensation. The novel therefore does not suggest that Mira received nothing after her original accident; her continuing financial pressure arises from lost earnings, additional support choices and consequential costs beyond the narrative's simplified account of statutory recovery.
